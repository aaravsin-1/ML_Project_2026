# Evaluating and Backtesting an Execution-Cost-Reduction Strategy on Historical LOB Data

Scope: benchmarks and metrics, how realistic a simulation on 1-second, 10-level snapshots can be, statistical pitfalls, and a concrete design for comparing 1 BTC parent orders (buy and sell) over a 5-minute window on Coinbase BTC.
Project context (checked in the repo): `src/execution/simulator.py` already has taker book-walking with wall-clock latency (`arrival_index`), a conservative passive trade-through rule (`passive_fill_index`: "we assume we sit at the back of the queue, so a resting bid only fills once the best bid trades *below* our price"), and maker rebate booking. Note: line ~127 fills any quantity beyond the top 10 levels at the last visible ask. That is optimistic if a 1 BTC order ever exhausts the visible depth.

## 1. Benchmarks and metrics: implementation shortfall, arrival mid, VWAP/TWAP, effective vs realized spread, markouts, bp savings, and how TCA practitioners compare algorithms

### Takeaway
The main metric should be implementation shortfall (IS) against the arrival mid, signed so that a positive value is a cost, in bp, with fees included and any unfilled remainder charged as opportunity cost. Report markouts / realized spread as a secondary diagnostic of adverse selection. VWAP and TWAP should only be used as noise-reduced secondary benchmarks, never as the objective, because they can be gamed and they absorb the timing effect a signal-timed policy is trying to capture.

### Cited Findings
- Perold (1988) defined implementation shortfall as "Return/profits on a paper portfolio – Return/profits on actual portfolio". Paper trades are assumed to execute at benchmark prices, and real trades at market prices. — [Hasbrouck, NYU Stern class notes "Trading costs" (2015)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- Perold's original IS covers explicit costs (commissions net of rebates, taxes), implicit costs (spread and impact relative to the benchmark), **opportunity costs ("the penalty associated with not completing intended trades")**, and **delay**. In current practice IS is usually computed per order as (trade price − benchmark) for buys and (benchmark − trade price) for sells. That version leaves out commissions, delay and opportunity cost, "which are sometimes tabulated separately". — [Hasbrouck notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- For a parent order worked over time, the execution price is the share-weighted average of the child fills. Worked example: a buy executed at 20.05/20.06/20.08 against a 20.035 arrival mid gives IS = $0.032/share. The NBBO midpoint at decision time is "probably the most common benchmark"; VWAP is "probably the second most common choice". — [Hasbrouck notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- Effective cost = p − m (buy) or m − p (sell), with m the prevailing mid. Realized cost uses the mid 5 minutes after the trade (m5) and is interpreted as the profit of the liquidity provider on the other side. Effective and realized spread are 2× the corresponding costs. — [Hasbrouck notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- SEC Rule 605 defines effective spread as 2×|execution price − NBBO mid at execution|. Realized spread uses the mid 15 s or 1 min after execution, and the 2024 amendments add more horizons. Price impact = effective half-spread − realized half-spread, which is the post-trade mid move, i.e. the markout. — [Schwab Rule 605 guide](https://schwab.com/legal/sec-605); [SEC 2024 Rule 605 fact sheet](https://www.sec.gov:443/files/34-99679-fact-sheet.pdf)
- **The limit-order trap (Hasbrouck):** submit 100 buy market orders and 100 buy limits at the bid. The market orders average +$0.05 effective cost and the filled limits average −$0.05, which suggests "we should use more limit orders". But limit buys fail exactly when "the market price has moved up, and the limit order is left behind". There is an opportunity cost, and one fix is to impute a fill for unexecuted orders at a later price (end of day or close; cf. TSE "Funari" orders). — [Hasbrouck notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann & Li (2005) measure cost on 29,509 filtered Citigroup US equity orders (from 682,562 raw). S0 = the mid just before the first fill; realized impact J = (S̄ − S0)/S0; permanent impact I = (S_post − S0)/S0, with S_post the mid **30 minutes after the last execution** ("for shorter time intervals the regressed values depend on the time lag, and at about this level the variation stops"). Costs are signed so that positive means adverse, and "since volatility is a very large contributor to their values, they are almost equally likely to have either sign". Fitted temporary-impact exponent β = 0.600 ± 0.038 (the 3/5 law); permanent impact roughly linear (α = 0.891 ± 0.10). Residuals are "extremely fat-tailed". — [Almgren et al. 2005, "Direct Estimation of Equity Market Impact" (PDF)](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/costestim.pdf)
- The Bayesian TCA paper (Kolm-style broker ranking, arXiv 1904.01566) uses IS = (Arrival − P̄)/P̄ × S × 10⁴ bp (S = +1 buy, −1 sell; in that sign convention negative means a cost). VWAP and PWP20 are defined the same way with the benchmark swapped. Reversion is Rev5m = (VWAP over the 5 min after the last fill − P_lastfill)/P_lastfill × S × 10⁴. The paper treats IS and reversion as primary, with VWAP/PWP secondary, and argues that IS plus reversion discriminate between strategies better than VWAP. It says "The reversion benchmark cannot be gamed". That claim comes from this paper alone and is not a consensus. Costs are modelled as asymmetric Laplace (fat-tailed and skewed), with heteroskedastic scale driven by size/ADV, participation, volatility and spread, and outliers beyond |IS| > 500 bp are dropped. — [Bayesian TCA and Ranking of Broker Algorithms (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.01566)
- VWAP can be gamed by trading aggressively early. Madhavan (2002) notes that VWAP-type benchmarks are especially open to gaming. — [search summary of arXiv 1904.01566 and Madhavan 2002](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/TP_Spring_2002_Madhavan.pdf) (Madhavan PDF not opened in full; treat as secondary)
- In crypto there is no consolidated tape, so "a single exchange's price is an incomplete benchmark", and maker/taker tiers and rebates "must be normalized to compare true cost". — [CoinRoutes, TCA for digital assets](https://coinroutes.com/cn/academy/transaction-cost-analysis-tca-digital-assets/)
- Eisler & Muhle-Karbe (2024) show that slippage measured against **mid prices sampled during execution** (cost relative to TWAP of the mid), instead of the arrival price, removes most market noise while keeping the linear cost term. This raised the signal-to-noise ratio about 6-7x in their E-mini calibration. — [Eisler & Muhle-Karbe, arXiv 2405.18936](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18936)

### Inferences
- **Recommended primary metric (per parent order i, policy π):**
  `IS_bp(i,π) = s_i × (P̄_exec(i,π) − M_arr(i)) / M_arr(i) × 1e4 + fees_bp(i,π) + OppCost_bp(i,π)`, with s = +1 for buys and −1 for sells, and positive meaning a cost. `M_arr` is the mid of the snapshot at the decision time (before any latency is applied, so that latency shows up as a cost). Any quantity left unfilled at the 5-minute deadline must be forced through a market order (book walk at T + latency). That turns opportunity cost into realized cost. Don't drop unfilled orders or score them only on the filled part.
- **Savings in bp** = paired difference `Δ_i = IS(i, baseline) − IS(i, policy)` on the *same* parent order (same start time, side and size). Report mean Δ, median Δ, a bootstrap CI, and the share of orders with Δ > 0.
- **Secondary metrics:** (a) IS vs interval TWAP-of-mid over the order's own window (low noise, isolates "how well did I trade within the window", but has no credit for timing); (b) taker vs maker share and fill rate; (c) child-level markouts at +1 s, +5 s, +10 s, +30 s, +60 s; for passive fills, a negative markout means adverse selection (cf. Section 2); (d) post-completion reversion at +1 min and +5 min after the last child (high reversion means the policy pushed the price, though simulated replay contains no impact, see Section 2); (e) cost decomposition: half-spread paid, book walking beyond L1, fees, drift during the window (timing), and the forced-cross cost on the remainder.
- Don't use interval VWAP as the headline benchmark. A timing policy that delays buying until the model predicts a down-tick lowers IS vs arrival, but its VWAP slippage barely changes because VWAP moves with the same path. VWAP also lets you score well by matching volume, whatever the cost.
- On Coinbase BTC the quoted spread is usually one tick on a ~$100k price, i.e. well under 0.01 bp. At that size, **fees and book walking dominate**, and the 0.7 bp predictive edge has to beat fee differences between maker and taker. Fee assumptions must be explicit and tier-specific, and results should be shown both with and without fees.

### Gaps
- I could not open Perold (1988) itself (JPM paywalled), so the definition comes from Hasbrouck's notes. Kissell's textbook (*The Science of Algorithmic Trading and Portfolio Management*) was not opened. Its I-Star model and its recommendation to compare algorithms using normalized cost (cost/σ) and Z-scores of cost vs pre-trade estimate are from memory and unverified here.
- I found no published practitioner write-up (Quantitative Brokers, Abel Noser, BestEx Research) that I could open on markout horizons for crypto. The horizons suggested above are inferences.

## 2. Simulation realism with historical snapshot data: impact feedback, passive fill rules, adverse selection, latency, partial fills, hidden liquidity

### Takeaway
Replaying historical snapshots can't model your own impact, and it can't tell where you sit in the queue. Every passive fill rule therefore puts a bias on the cost estimate. "Touch" fills are strongly optimistic. "Trade-through", which assumes you are at the back of the queue (what the project uses), is conservative on fill *rate* but still shows adverse selection correctly. Probabilistic queue models sit in between. The honest approach is to report results under at least two fill rules and present them as a bracket. For a 1 BTC taker order, the remaining impact concern is that liquidity you consume isn't replenished in the replay.

### Cited Findings
- hftbacktest documentation: in a replay, "your order cannot make any changes to the simulated market, no market impact is considered". Orders must be small enough not to move the market, and results must be validated live. In its default exchange model, liquidity-taking orders "will be fully executed at the best" regardless of displayed size ("may cause unrealistic fill simulations if you attempt to execute a large quantity"). In the partial-fill model they walk the displayed book, but "the best price and quantity do not change after your fill", so repeated child orders can consume the same liquidity again. — [hftbacktest docs, Order Fill](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/order_fill.rst.txt)
- hftbacktest fill conditions: a buy limit fills fully if its price is ≥ best ask, or **strictly above** a sell trade price (trade-through). At exactly the trade price, it fills only if the order is at the front of the queue (by the model's estimate). It "currently only supports Market-By-Price", and without Market-By-Order data, queue position "must be estimated through modeling". — [hftbacktest docs, Order Fill](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/order_fill.rst.txt)
- Queue models: `RiskAverseQueueModel` advances your position "only if a trade happens at the price". All cancellations are assumed to come from behind you, which makes it "the most conservative" model. `ProbQueueModel` splits each depth decrease between the queue ahead and behind with probability f(back)/(f(back)+f(front)), using f(x)=x^n (power) or log variants. The docs recommend calibrating the choice against live fills. — [hftbacktest Probability Queue Models tutorial](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorials/Probability%20Queue%20Models.html)
- Databento's practitioner write-up (only the search summary was available, page returned 403) estimates queue position from MBP with a parametric cancellation-allocation model, validated against MBO. In MBO data, cancels mostly come from the back of the queue. With MBP-10, the estimate becomes censored once the price moves beyond the visible 10 levels. — [Databento blog (search summary only)](https://medium.databento.com/getting-queue-position-with-l2-and-order-book-data-from-databento-d3f8039b2515)
- **Adverse selection, measured on crypto:** live minimum-size maker orders on Binance BTC perpetual show "a negative correlation between maker fill likelihood and post-fill returns". A top-of-queue order fills "with probability 1" if the next move goes against it. Fill probability ranges from ~30% to >90% depending on queue imbalance. Average 1-second markouts range from −0.06 bp (front of queue, favorable imbalance) to −1.16 bp (back of queue, large queues), with an overall average of about −0.8 bp (−0.3 bp net of rebate). The authors argue that synthetic-order backtests can't guarantee realizable fills "even with full L3 data", that tracking queue position from L2 is "impossible", and that >95% of maker orders are cancelled, which biases fill rates inferred from other people's orders. — [Albers et al., "The Market Maker's Dilemma", arXiv 2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)
- Lalor & Swishchuk: many backtests simulate price paths and order arrivals independently. "Excluding adverse fills and non-adverse fill probabilities can significantly inflate the performance of a trading strategy." — [Lalor & Swishchuk, "Market Simulation under Adverse Selection", arXiv 2409.12721](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12721v2) (summary from search result; not opened in full)
- Fill-probability research avoids selection bias by placing synthetic limit orders **uniformly over time** rather than on a strategy's own timing. — [Columbia deep-LOB fill-probability paper (search summary)](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/citation_file_upload/deep-lob-2021.pdf)
- Latency raises adverse selection for limit orders. Lehalle & Mounjid's order-placement model is calibrated and backtested on NASDAQ data with latency included. — [Lehalle & Mounjid, arXiv 1610.00261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.00261) (abstract-level only)

### Inferences (bias of each fill rule on 1-s, 10-level snapshots plus aggregated per-second buy/sell volume)
- **Touch rule** (fill when best ask ≤ your bid price, or any trade at your price): strongly *optimistic*. It assumes you are first in the queue and gives you fills that in reality went to orders ahead of you, especially when the price touches and bounces back (the favorable paths). It understates adverse selection, because the fills it adds are mostly benign ones. Don't use it as a headline number.
- **Trade-through with back-of-queue** (the project's current rule: fill only when the best bid moves below your price, i.e. the level is fully consumed): *conservative on fill rate*. You miss fills where volume traded at your level ate the queue without the price moving, which are usually the more benign fills. The fills that remain are mostly **adversely selected**: by construction the price has just moved through you, which is the worst-case version of the Market Maker's Dilemma pattern. Net effect: passive strategies look somewhat worse than reality, which is acceptable for a claim of the form "policy X saves at least Y bp". One subtlety: a quote-based trade-through on 1-s snapshots can't distinguish "traded through" from "the bid was cancelled and re-quoted lower within the second". Cancellation-driven gaps would be counted as fills that never happened, a small optimistic bias in the other direction. Requiring nonzero aggressive sell volume in that second (from the aggregated volume column) removes most of this.
- **Volume-at-price queue model** (place the order at the back of the displayed queue Q at post time, decrement your queue-ahead by aggressive volume in your direction each second, fill once the cumulative volume exceeds Q + your size): in between. With per-second *aggregated* buy/sell volume you don't know trade prices, so a practical version assigns all of the second's aggressive sell volume to the best bid. That over-credits fills when trades happened at multiple levels. Optionally, cancellations can also shrink the queue ahead with probability proportional to position (hftbacktest power model, n≈2-3).
- **Recommendation:** report passive-involving policies under (a) the current trade-through rule (the conservative bound) and (b) a volume-queue model (central estimate). If a conclusion only holds under the optimistic rule, it is not a finding.
- **Impact feedback:** for taker child orders the replay makes the visible book reappear after each child. Mitigations: (i) keep a "consumed liquidity" ledger so that a level depleted by your child at t is reduced at t+1…t+k (e.g. exponential refill with a half-life of a few seconds); (ii) add a parametric temporary-impact term (square-root or 3/5-power in participation, cf. Almgren et al.) as a sensitivity check; (iii) check that 1 BTC never needs more than 10 levels. The current "fill remainder at last ask" fallback understates cost when it triggers, so count how often it does.
- **Latency:** apply decision → exchange latency to both the decision snapshot and the fill snapshot. With 1-s snapshots, any latency below 1 s means "next snapshot", and that is a material assumption: it gives the signal one full second of staleness. Run a latency sensitivity check (0, 1, 2 s) because the signal edge decays at 10-60 s horizons.
- **Signal feature timing:** the features at snapshot t must use only data stamped ≤ t. Aggregated "volume per second" columns are especially risky if the bucket is labelled with its start time but contains trades up to t+1. Verify how the bucket is labelled before trusting OFI features.
- **Hidden liquidity / partial fills:** Coinbase has no hidden or iceberg orders on its public L2 feed as far as I know (unverified). Partial passive fills should be allowed under the volume-queue model. Under trade-through, a full fill is reasonable for 1 BTC only if the traded-through volume ≥ your residual size.

### Gaps
- I found no academic paper that benchmarks L2 queue-estimation rules against L3 ground truth. The Databento article was the closest source and could not be opened.
- I found no source quantifying the snapshot-sampling bias specific to 1-second sampling (versus event-level data) for fill simulation. The inferences above are reasoning, not measured.
- I did not verify Coinbase's current fee tiers or whether Coinbase supports hidden orders. Both should be checked before finalising the fee assumptions.

## 3. Statistical pitfalls: overlapping windows, look-ahead, multiple testing, small samples, regime dependence, paired tests, required sample size

### Takeaway
Execution-cost differences are tiny compared with price noise, so the experiment has to be **paired** (all policies on the identical start times), and inference should use block or cluster bootstrap over non-overlapping time blocks. Policy parameters should be chosen on validation only, with the number of variants tried reported (PBO / deflated-Sharpe logic). With arrival-mid IS on 5-minute BTC windows, an *unpaired* 0.5 bp detection needs thousands of independent orders, while a well-paired design can need only hundreds.

### Cited Findings
- Eisler & Muhle-Karbe (2024): with arrival-price slippage, "the means are much lower than the corresponding standard deviations". For a realistic N = 1,000 orders, the t-statistic of the linear cost term is only ≈ 0.54, rising to ≈ 3.57 (unconditional) or 7.42 (conditional) when slippage is measured against mids during execution. For impact, t ≈ 0.95 → 7.32 with their reweighted estimator. "Large datasets of thousands of orders are required" under the standard approach. — [Eisler & Muhle-Karbe, arXiv 2405.18936](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18936)
- Bayesian TCA paper: "the mean implementation shortfall is small relative to the variance". Costs are fat-tailed (asymmetric Laplace), so sample means converge slowly. The authors use heteroskedastic models and hierarchical pooling. — [arXiv 1904.01566 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.01566)
- Almgren et al.: cost residuals are "extremely fat-tailed, as is normal for returns distributions on short time intervals", and individual-order costs are "almost equally likely to have either sign". — [Almgren et al. 2005](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/costestim.pdf)
- An individual (not peer-reviewed) algo-wheel simulation: against arrival, residual cost SD ≈ 64 bp, which needs ~63,775 orders per broker to detect 1 bp at 80% power. Against interval VWAP, SD ≈ 9 bp, needing ~1,283 orders. — [m-ichaels/algo-wheel GitHub](https://github.com/m-ichaels/algo-wheel) (equity, multi-day orders; shown only to illustrate how much benchmark choice changes the required sample size)
- Algo-wheel rankings only mean something if they normalise for differences in order flow across brokers (CFA Institute TCA guidance, as cited). — [AlgoTrader, Execution algorithms](https://algotrader.ch/resources/execution-algorithms/) (secondary citation)
- Probability of Backtest Overfitting (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, Zhu) uses combinatorially symmetric cross-validation to estimate the probability that the in-sample best configuration underperforms the median out-of-sample. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado 2014) corrects for selection bias under multiple testing and for non-normal returns. — [SSRN 2326253 (PBO)](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253); [SSRN 2460551 (DSR)](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551); [R `pbo` package vignette](https://ftp.fau.de/cran/web/packages/pbo/vignettes/pbo.html)

### Inferences
- **Sample-size arithmetic (paired design).** For a two-sided test at α = 0.05 with 80% power, n ≈ 7.85 × (σ_Δ / δ)², where σ_Δ is the SD of the *paired* per-order difference.
  - δ = 0.5 bp: σ_Δ = 2 bp → n ≈ 126; σ_Δ = 5 bp → n ≈ 785; σ_Δ = 10 bp → n ≈ 3,140.
  - δ = 1.0 bp: σ_Δ = 2 → 32; 5 → 197; 10 → 785.
  - Measure σ_Δ from a pilot of about 200 orders on the validation split and plug it in. Inflate n for fat tails (bootstrap CIs, not t-tests) and for dependence (effective n < n when start times are close).
- **Rough σ for BTC (illustration; assumes ~50% annualised vol, which should be replaced with the data's realised vol):** per-minute σ ≈ 0.5/√525,600 ≈ 6.9 bp. A TWAP over T = 5 min has an average-price deviation from the arrival mid with SD ≈ σ√(T/3) ≈ 8.9 bp. An immediate market order has almost no price-path variance vs arrival. So *unpaired* TWAP-vs-market comparisons need n ≈ 7.85 × (8.9/0.5)² ≈ 2,500 orders for 0.5 bp. Pairing TWAP against a signal-timed variant of TWAP on the same path cancels most of the shared drift. σ_Δ then comes only from the timing deviations, which is why the signal policy should be built as a *perturbation of TWAP* (same schedule envelope, signal shifts children within the window) rather than as a different execution shape.
- **The comparison against immediate market order is different in kind.** IS(TWAP) − IS(MO) is dominated by the 5-minute drift, which has mean ~0 but SD ~9 bp. TWAP "saves" spread and depth cost but takes on timing risk. Report mean *and* SD (a risk-adjusted cost, e.g. mean + λ·SD, as in Almgren-Chriss), not just the mean.
- **Overlapping windows:** random start times with 5-min windows will overlap. Overlapping parent orders share price paths, so their costs are correlated. Fixes: (a) draw start times on a non-overlapping grid (one start per 5-10 min block), or (b) allow overlap but bootstrap by blocks (e.g. 1-hour or 1-day blocks) and report a block-bootstrap CI for mean Δ. Also make sure no parent-order window crosses a split boundary or purge gap.
- **Look-ahead:** (i) the model's labels (10-60 s forward returns) overlap the test windows, so training must be purged and embargoed relative to the *execution* test period, which the project already does; (ii) feature timestamps (see Section 2); (iii) any normalisation statistics (z-scores of OFI, volatility scaling) must be fit on train only; (iv) the threshold that maps prediction to action must be chosen on validation.
- **Multiple testing over policy parameters** (threshold, max delay, slice count, limit offset, latency): every variant you try is a trial. Freeze a small grid (e.g. ≤ 20 configurations) and select on validation. Report test results only for the chosen one plus TWAP/MO baselines, and also the full test distribution across the grid (or PBO via CSCV over time blocks). With 4 BTC segments, apply CSCV with the segments or sub-blocks as partitions.
- **Regime dependence:** stratify results by realised volatility tercile, spread/depth tercile, segment, and hour of day (UTC). A 0.7 bp edge that comes entirely from one segment or high-vol regime is fragile. Report per-segment Δ with CIs and check that the sign agrees.
- **Buy and sell symmetry:** run both sides on the same start times. Over a trending segment, buy-side timing gains are mirrored by sell-side losses for drift-driven policies. A real signal should give positive Δ on *both* sides, so report them separately and pooled.
- **Paired tests to use:** a paired bootstrap (block-resampled) of mean Δ; a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the median as a robustness check; a sign test for the share of orders with Δ > 0. Use Holm correction across the 3-4 policy-vs-baseline comparisons.

- I found no published estimate of per-order paired cost-difference variance for crypto execution at 5-minute horizons. The σ values above are illustrative and must be measured from the user's data.

## 4. Fair-comparison design: market order vs TWAP vs signal-timed vs passive-then-cross, 1 BTC buy/sell within 5 minutes, Coinbase 1-s 10-level snapshots

### Takeaway
Use a common random set of (start time, side) parent orders drawn only from the held-out test period. Run every policy on exactly those orders with the same simulator, latency, fees and forced completion at the deadline. Score everything against the arrival mid at decision time, and report paired differences against TWAP (the primary baseline) and against the immediate market order. Conclusions should hold under both the conservative trade-through fill rule and a central volume-queue fill rule.

### Cited Findings
- Arrival mid at decision time is the standard pre-trade benchmark. Opportunity cost of unfilled limit orders must be charged, e.g. by imputing a fill at a later market price. — [Hasbrouck notes](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf)
- Synthetic orders should be placed uniformly in time to avoid selection bias in fill statistics. — [Columbia deep-LOB fill-probability paper](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/citation_file_upload/deep-lob-2021.pdf)
- Maker fills on BTC are adversely selected, with an average 1-s markout of about −0.8 bp, comparable in size to the user's 0.7 bp predictive edge. — [arXiv 2502.18625](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18625v2)
- Measuring slippage against mids during execution (TWAP-of-mid) instead of arrival raises the signal-to-noise ratio about 6-7x. — [arXiv 2405.18936](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18936)
- Replay simulators cannot model impact. Large taker orders fill unrealistically if displayed liquidity isn't depleted. — [hftbacktest docs](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/order_fill.rst.txt)

### Inferences: concrete recommendations
1. **Order generation.** From the *test* split only (after purge/embargo), draw start times uniformly at random, keeping only those whose full window [t0, t0 + 5 min + latency + markout horizon] lies inside one contiguous segment with no data gaps. Use a non-overlapping grid (e.g. one random start per 10-minute block) for the primary analysis, and fully random overlapping starts with block bootstrap as a robustness check. For each start, create **both** a buy and a sell of 1 BTC. The same orders (seeded RNG, saved to disk) go to every policy.
2. **Benchmark.** M_arr = mid of the snapshot at t0 (the decision time). Latency is a cost and is charged against this. Primary metric is IS_bp as defined in Section 1, including fees, with any unfilled remainder force-crossed at t0 + 300 s (+ latency) by book walk. Secondary metrics: IS vs TWAP-of-mid over [t0, t0+300 s]; the cost decomposition; child markouts at +1/5/10/30/60 s; reversion at +60 s and +300 s after completion.
3. **Policies (all must finish by the deadline):**
   - **P0 Immediate MO:** book-walk 1 BTC at t0 + latency. Has nearly zero timing risk and serves as the lower bound for risk.
   - **P1 TWAP-taker:** N equal children (e.g. 10 × 0.1 BTC every 30 s, or 30 × 1/30 BTC every 10 s), each a taker book-walk. Primary baseline. The child grid should start at a random phase offset so that it doesn't line up with any periodic artefacts.
   - **P2 Signal-timed TWAP:** the same N children, but each child has a slot [k·Δ, (k+1)·Δ). Inside its slot it executes at the first second where the model's predicted adverse move for this side exceeds threshold θ, and otherwise at the slot end. A catch-up rule keeps the cumulative executed quantity within ±1 child of the TWAP schedule. Because the schedule envelope stays the same, the paired variance against P1 stays small and any gain comes from the signal.
   - **P3 Passive-then-cross (no signal):** post each child at the near touch (bid for buys) at slot start, and cross the residual at slot end. Evaluate it under both fill rules.
   - **P4 Signal-gated passive-then-cross:** post passively only when the model predicts a favourable or neutral move (low adverse-selection risk), and otherwise take immediately. This tests whether the signal avoids the adverse fills seen in the Market Maker's Dilemma result.
   - **Control: P2 with a shuffled or lagged signal** (e.g. predictions permuted within the day, or lagged by 5 min). This should give Δ ≈ 0 against P1. If it doesn't, the simulator or the schedule has a structural bias.
4. **Parameter selection.** Tune θ, N, and the passive offset on the *validation* split using the same protocol. Freeze a grid of at most about 20 configurations, record all of them, and report PBO/CSCV across segments or time blocks. Report test results once.
5. **Inference.** Primary contrasts: P2 − P1, P4 − P3, P3 − P1, P1 − P0 (all paired, per order, in bp). Use block bootstrap CIs (blocks = 1 h or 1 day), Wilcoxon signed-rank, and a sign test, with Holm correction across contrasts. Estimate σ_Δ from a validation pilot and size the test sample with n ≈ 7.85(σ_Δ/δ)². Aim for δ = 0.5 bp with at least 80% power, and state the minimum detectable effect if the data is too short.
6. **Realism sensitivities** (each is a re-run on the same orders): latency of 0/1/2 s; fill rule trade-through vs volume-queue; with and without a liquidity-depletion ledger or temporary impact term; fees at the current tier, a lower tier, and zero; order size 0.5/1/5 BTC to show where the visible-depth limits bind. Count how often the "remainder beyond L10 filled at last ask" fallback triggers.
7. **Stratification and reporting.** Report per side (buy/sell), per segment, per volatility tercile, and per spread/depth tercile. Show the distribution (violin or CDF) of per-order Δ, the fill rate and maker share for P3/P4, the average markout curve of passive fills against taker fills, and a cost-vs-risk plot (mean IS vs SD of IS) for P0-P4.
8. **Sanity checks.** (a) P1 − P0 should be roughly equal to the expected book-walk saving minus a zero-mean drift; (b) mean IS of buy plus sell for a drift-neutral policy should be about 2× the average half-spread plus fees; (c) the shuffled-signal control should give Δ ≈ 0; (d) passive fills under trade-through should have negative short-horizon markouts on average. If they are positive, look for look-ahead in the fill logic.
