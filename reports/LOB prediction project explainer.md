# Order-book signals survive the audit, not the fees

This project (repository `ML_Project_2026`) asks two linked questions. First, can the direction of the next few hundred milliseconds to tens of seconds of price movement be predicted from the state of a limit order book (LOB)? Second, does that prediction still make money once you pay the spread, exchange fees and latency? With every experiment now finished, the answer is **yes, it can be predicted, a little and briefly, and no, it does not make money as a spread-crossing strategy**. On the synthetic market (5 seeds), LightGBM on 27 microstructure features reaches **macro-F1 0.584 ± 0.012**, against **0.519** for an honest "repeat the last realized label" rule. Crossing the spread leaves **24.6%** of the frictionless profit for LightGBM and **5.8%** for DeepLOB ([exp A](../artifacts/experiment_a_results.csv); [exp D](../artifacts/experiment_d_execution_strategies.csv)). On real Coinbase BTC data, across four ~3-day segments at a 20-second horizon, LightGBM scores **0.467 ± 0.021** against **0.413 ± 0.021** for momentum. It beats momentum in 4/4 segments, ties Random Forest, and beats DeepLOB (**0.401 ± 0.048**, *below* the momentum baseline) in 4/4 ([BTC exp A](../artifacts/btc/experiment_a_results.csv)). That edge is worth only about **$0.52 per trade at the mid**. A 4 bp taker fee costs about **$2.41 per trade**, so LightGBM's frictionless **+$8,055 per segment becomes −$36,394** once it crosses the spread ([BTC exp D](../artifacts/btc/experiment_d_execution_strategies.csv)). On FI-2010 (3 seeds), the project's DeepLOB reproduces the published accuracy within **0.7–2.3 points**. Given the same price history DeepLOB sees, though, a plain logistic regression beats it at short horizons (**86.1% vs 83.1%** at k = 10, 78.3% vs 74.1% at k = 20), while DeepLOB wins clearly at k = 50 and 100. The ranking depends on inputs and horizon, not on deep learning being better in general ([FI-2010 summary](../artifacts/fi2010/fi2010_setup2_summary.csv)). On Optiver Realized Volatility, the 1st-place solution's "neighbour" features earn about 0.009 RMSPE (41%) of their improvement from future information, and shuffled cross-validation looks 0.021 better than honest forward validation ([RVP summary](../artifacts/optiver_rvp/rvp_summary.csv)). On Optiver Trading at the Close, LightGBM improves on predicting zero by **2.4%** MAE (5.9936 → 5.8476), and 12-day retraining adds only about 0.1% more ([Close holdout](../artifacts/optiver_close/close_holdout_results.csv)). Before any of this, an audit found ten bugs that had produced a fake headline. A momentum baseline peeked at the future (macro-F1 0.965), and a "DeepLOB" latency curve was really LightGBM run twice. Fixing them reversed the thesis from "speed beats model complexity" to "**signal quality beats speed, and fees beat both**" ([commit 7767a7d](../README.md)). That conclusion matches the most careful recent literature, which finds LOB predictability real but short-lived and rarely tradable after costs ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915); [Briola et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/); [TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Follow-up experiments (E–K) on BTC and on one day of Nasdaq event data (LOBSTER) sharpen it. No confidence filter, regime or market-making scheme beats costs on BTC. Large-tick stocks are predictable at 1–5 s, where a one-parameter micro-price rule matches LightGBM. Splitting the BTC round trip shows that even a perfect direction forecast could not cover the 8 bp taker round trip. The [final report](../submission/PROJECT_REPORT.md) turns this into a rule for balancing accuracy, latency and cost.

## How to explain this in two minutes

If you have to explain the project out loud, this script follows the logic in the order a listener needs it. "Every exchange keeps a list of people waiting to buy and sell at each price. That list is the limit order book. When the queue of sellers at the best price is much shorter than the queue of buyers, the next price move is more likely to be up, because only a few shares need to trade or cancel to clear the sellers. Researchers have shown this empirically for Nasdaq stocks ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)), and DeepLOB-style neural networks try to learn it automatically ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). My project builds one pipeline that turns order-book snapshots into features such as order-flow imbalance, queue imbalance and the micro-price. It trains a ladder of models from naive rules up to DeepLOB and a transformer, all scored on exactly the same test rows. Then it pushes their buy and sell signals through an execution simulator that pays the spread, the exchange fee and a realistic delay. The first version of my project looked spectacular, but an audit showed that its best baseline was secretly reading the future, and its 'fast model beats slow model' chart was a fabrication. After fixing ten such bugs, the honest story is this. The models do predict direction better than chance and better than a naive rule, but only a little, and only for a few seconds. By 100 seconds on Bitcoin the best model no longer beats 'repeat the last move'. On real Bitcoin data, a gradient-boosted tree model on hand-built order-book features matches or beats every deep network I tried, and DeepLOB actually falls below the naive rule. On the standard FI-2010 academic benchmark my DeepLOB reproduces the published numbers within about two points. But once I gave a plain logistic regression the same price history DeepLOB sees, it beat DeepLOB at short horizons; DeepLOB only wins clearly at longer ones. So which model wins depends on the inputs and the horizon. The money question has a clear answer. On Bitcoin the edge is about 52 cents a trade and the fee is about $2.41, so trading on every signal loses about $36,000 per three-day test. Speed matters far less than signal quality and fees: in my synthetic market, DeepLOB's extra 2 milliseconds of thinking cost about $10, while its weaker signals cost about $350. Latency still matters, though, and most for the best signals: the most confident Bitcoin signals lose a third of their edge just from acting one second late. I also used two Optiver Kaggle competitions. In one, I measured how much of the winning solution's improvement came from information about the future. In the other, I beat predicting zero by about 2.4%, which is roughly the size of edge the winners had. The lesson is that 'statistically predictable' and 'economically exploitable' are different claims, and a credible project has to test both."

The one-sentence version: *the order book does predict short-term moves, but the prediction is worth less than the cost of acting on it unless you trade far more selectively than every signal.*

## The order book is a queue-depletion machine

An LOB is a continuous double auction. Buyers' resting limit orders (bids) and sellers' resting orders (asks) sit at discrete prices that are multiples of the **tick size**, and they are matched by **price-time priority**: a better price executes first, and at the same price the earliest order executes first ([Gould et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1012.0349); [Xu, Gould & Howison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). The best bid b and best ask a define the **spread** a − b and the **mid-price** (a + b)/2. "Level k" is the k-th best price on a side, and its **depth** is the total size resting there. A market order "eats" the best queue. When that queue is empty, the best price steps to the next level.

The tick size matters more than beginners expect. For **large-tick** assets the spread is almost always one tick, and Eisler, Bouchaud and Kockelkoren report that events changing the best price make up only about **3%** of events ([arXiv:0904.0900](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0900)). The mid can only move when a best queue is depleted, so the relative size of the two best queues is the key state variable. For **small-tick** assets the spread is several ticks wide and much of the action happens inside it. On Nasdaq, only **0.6–1.6%** of order flow lands inside the spread for large-tick stocks, versus **14–27%** for small-tick ones ([Xu, Gould & Howison, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). This is why the project's synthetic generator has a large-tick regime (spread 1 tick, mean queue 800) and a small-tick regime (spread 3 ticks, queue 120). Its validation report also includes a "small-tick transfer" check ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)).

### Queue imbalance and the micro-price turn queues into a probability

The simplest predictor is **queue imbalance**, I = (n_b − n_a)/(n_b + n_a) ∈ [−1, 1], where n_b and n_a are the best-bid and best-ask volumes. Gould and Bonart fit P(up | I) = 1/(1 + e^−(x₀ + x₁I)) on 10 Nasdaq stocks over 2014. They found a "strongly statistically significant relationship" for every stock. Binary classification improved on a coin flip by **50–60% for large-tick** and **10–30% for small-tick** stocks ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Here is a worked example (illustrative arithmetic, not from a paper): bid 900 shares, ask 100 shares gives I = +0.8. Only 100 shares must trade or cancel to lift the ask, against 900 to break the bid, so "up" is more likely.

The mid-price ignores this information. The **weighted mid**, W = I′·P_a + (1 − I′)·P_b with I′ = Q_b/(Q_b + Q_a), corrects it. It weights the ask by the *bid* size, so a heavy bid pulls the estimate toward the ask. This is exactly the **WAP** used in the Optiver competitions: WAP = (bid_price·ask_size + ask_price·bid_size)/(bid_size + ask_size) ([BulletTech writeup](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/BulletTech/BulletTech/main/docs/Main_Course/Machine_Learning/2021-08-27-Kaggle-optiver.md); [Erdős slides](https://erdosinstitute.org/_files/ugd/be9ca7_e4c07cbf1fc445d69dc482e5884016ca.pdf)). Stoikov's **micro-price** is the principled refinement: the expected long-run mid given the current imbalance and spread, P_micro = M + g(I, S), estimated with a Markov chain ([SSRN 2970694](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2970694); [code](https://github.com/sstoikov/microprice)). *Flag: the micro-price formula and the claim that it is a martingale come from background knowledge. The SSRN text could not be retrieved, so check them against the paper before quoting.* The project uses the simpler deviation (micro − mid) = I·S/2 as a feature ([README](../README.md)).

### Order-flow imbalance counts what changed, not what is resting

Queue imbalance is a snapshot. **Order-flow imbalance (OFI)**, from Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014), measures the *change* in supply and demand at the best quotes. For each quote update n:

```
e_n = 1{P^B_n >= P^B_{n-1}}·q^B_n − 1{P^B_n <= P^B_{n-1}}·q^B_{n-1}
    − 1{P^A_n <= P^A_{n-1}}·q^A_n + 1{P^A_n >= P^A_{n-1}}·q^A_{n-1}
OFI_k = Σ e_n over the interval;   ΔP_k = β·OFI_k + ε_k;   β ∝ 1/depth
```

Intuitively, a new bid, a cancelled ask and a market buy all add positive pressure. A market sell and a cancelled bid of the same size count identically. On 50 S&P 500 stocks over 10-second intervals, mid changes are linear in OFI with an average **R² of 65%**, against **32%** for trade imbalance, and the slope is roughly inversely proportional to depth ([CKS 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). *Flag: the per-event formula above is the standard published form; the PDF math was garbled during research, so cross-check it against Eq. (1) of the paper.* The key caveat for a prediction project is that **this R² is contemporaneous**: it explains price changes using order flow from the same interval.

**Multi-level OFI (MLOFI)** applies the same bookkeeping at deeper levels. Out-of-sample fit improves with every added level, by about **65–75% (large-tick) and 15–30% (small-tick)** in RMSE ([Xu, Gould & Howison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). Because per-level OFIs are highly collinear, Cont, Cucuringu and Zhang compress the top 10 levels into the first principal component, "integrated OFI". That component explains **89.06%** of the variance and raises contemporaneous out-of-sample R² from **64.64% to 83.83%**. Yet the out-of-sample R² for *one-minute-ahead* returns is **negative** for every model ([CCZ, Tables 2, 5, 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)). This explain-versus-forecast gap runs through the whole project. The project computes OFI at all 10 levels and compresses it with a **train-only PCA** to 3 components, which is the leak-free version of CCZ's recipe ([pipeline](../src/microstructure/pipeline.py)).

### VPIN measures one-sided flow but carries a contested reputation

**VPIN** (Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara 2012) measures order-flow "toxicity", meaning how badly liquidity providers are being adversely selected. Trading is split into equal-*volume* buckets (a "volume clock"), and the absolute buy–sell imbalance is averaged over the last n buckets: VPIN = Σ|V^S_τ − V^B_τ|/(n·V) ([IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v25y2012i5p1457-1493.html)). Buys and sells are classified either trade by trade or by "bulk volume classification" (V^B = V·Z(ΔP/σ)). *Flag: the exact formulas are the standard statement; the primary RFS text was not accessible, though Andersen & Bondarenko confirm the structure.* Here is a worked example (illustrative arithmetic): with bucket size 1,000 and buy volumes 500, 800 and 900, the imbalances are 0, 600 and 800, so VPIN = 1,400/3,000 ≈ 0.47.

Present VPIN with its critique attached. Andersen and Bondarenko found that VPIN reached its historical high only **after** the 2010 Flash Crash. They show it is mechanically tied to trading intensity, and that its correlation with future volatility is no better than a version using **random** trade classification ([CREATES RP 2011-50](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf); [JFM 2014](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2014/vpin-and-the-flash-crash/)). The original authors replied that the critique attacks "a methodology we do not advocate" ([rejoinder](https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/118180)). The fair summary is that VPIN is a reasonable volume-time summary of signed flow plus activity, but not a validated crash or volatility forecaster. The project computes real volume-clock VPIN from the true-aggressor trade tape, mapped causally to each snapshot (`searchsorted(..., side="right") − 1`), and uses its train-period 90th percentile as a "toxic flow" gate for passive quoting ([vpin.py](../src/microstructure/vpin.py); [strategies](../src/execution/strategies.py)).

### Adverse selection explains why any of this predicts anything

Why should order flow predict prices at all? In **Glosten–Milgrom (1985)** a market maker faces some informed traders. A buy raises the probability that the asset is worth more, so ask = E[value | buy] and bid = E[value | sell], and the spread compensates for losing to the informed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3)). In **Kyle (1985)** prices respond linearly to net order flow, Δp = λ·y, and 1/λ is market depth ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913210)). *Flag: bibliographic details for both are from background knowledge; this is standard textbook material.* The CKS finding ΔP = β·OFI with β ∝ 1/depth is the empirical, quote-level cousin of Kyle's lambda ([CKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). The same logic explains why passive orders are dangerous. A resting bid gets filled exactly when a seller arrives, and sellers are disproportionately informed just before the price falls. That is why the project's passive maker earns almost nothing once fills are modelled honestly.

### Realized volatility is the target in the first Optiver dataset

The Optiver Realized Volatility competition uses WAP log returns r_t = log(WAP_t/WAP_{t−1}) and **realized volatility** RV = √(Σ r_t²) over a 10-minute bucket. It scores predictions by **RMSPE** = √(mean(((y − ŷ)/y)²)), which penalizes *relative* error ([competition evaluation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/overview/evaluation)). A participant showed that RMSPE equals RMSE weighted by 1/y², and 1/y² sample weights became standard practice ([discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/250324)). The project's LightGBM uses exactly those weights ([optiver_rvp.py](../src/experiments/optiver_rvp.py)). Volatility clusters, so "next RV = current RV" is a strong naive baseline. Optiver's own tutorial reports **RMSPE 0.341** for it on train ([tutorial](https://www.kaggle.com/code/jiashenliu/introduction-to-financial-concepts-and-data)).

## One pipeline scores seven models on identical test rows

The design principle, which came out of the audit, is that **every model is evaluated identically**. That means one data-prep module, features fit on training rows only, chronologically purged splits, the same test rows for tabular and sequence models, and the same class weighting. The README states the purpose as testing "whether predictions survive realistic execution" ([README](../README.md)). The original one-line spec framed the goal as finding "the boundary between theoretical alpha and realized profitability" ([project_research.md](../project_research.md)).

### Features: 27 tabular columns and one 40-column tensor

`MicrostructurePipeline.fit()` estimates the tick size, a decimal-precision normalizer and the MLOFI PCA (3 components) **on training snapshots only**. `transform()` then emits 27 tabular features: depth imbalance at levels 1, 3, 5 and 10; spread in ticks; micro-price deviation; bid and ask book slopes; VPIN; level-1 OFI; three OFI principal components; per-level MLOFI for levels 1–5; and lags 1, 2 and 5 of OFI, L1 imbalance and micro-price deviation. Separately it emits a normalized 40-column tensor (10 levels × bid/ask × price/size) for the sequence models, using 100-step windows ([pipeline.py](../src/microstructure/pipeline.py); [feature list](../artifacts/experiment_c_feature_importance.csv)). This mirrors the field's split between engineered order-flow features and raw-book deep learning. That split matters because Kolm, Turiel and Westray found models trained on OFI "generally beat" models trained on raw book states ([Kolm et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v33y2023i4p1044-1081.html)). DeepLOB's own convolutions are explicitly designed to rebuild micro-price and imbalance ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)).

### Labels: smoothed three-class direction with a FLAT band

The synthetic label compares a 5-event trailing mean of the mid at t+k with the mid at t. It is UP or DOWN beyond **α = 1.5 bp** and FLAT otherwise, and the last k labels are set to FLAT ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)). On BTC the threshold is set per horizon to the **1/3 quantile of |smoothed k-row return| over training rows only**, so one third of training labels are FLAT ([labels.py](../src/data/labels.py); [common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)). FI-2010 keeps its published labels: the mean of the next k mids versus the current mid, with a fixed ±0.002 threshold ([Ntakaris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03233)). That threshold was picked to balance classes at one horizon, "not … in accordance with its financial implication" ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). Smoothed labels deserve suspicion. Labels at t and t+1 share k−1 future mids, so they are highly autocorrelated. Lee (2024) shows that on a smoothed AAPL target DeepLOB scores **0.659** accuracy against **0.648** for a naive forecast that just reuses the lagged target ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14157v1)). This is why the project's honest momentum baseline is so important.

### Validation: purged chronological splits and a self-checking report

Splits are chronological 70/15/15. `purged_split_indices` drops the last k rows of train and of validation, because their labels overlap the next split, and drops the final k rows of the series, where labels are undefined. Training starts at row `SEQ_LEN − 1` so sequence and tabular models train on the same rows. `LOBSequenceDataset(target_indices=…)` builds each window to end exactly at its target row, using earlier rows as causal context ([lob_dataset.py](../src/data/lob_dataset.py); [common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)). This is the "purging" idea from López de Prado: drop training samples whose label windows overlap the test labels ([Wikipedia: purged CV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation)). The robustness script adds several checks: purged cross-validation with a 1% embargo, a permutation test, MCC, Cohen's kappa, a train–test generalization gap, lift over momentum, and transfer from large-tick to small-tick data. Each check is a `(name, value, condition, criterion)` tuple whose PASS/FAIL is computed, not hard-coded ([verify script](../scripts/verify_robustness_and_leakage.py); [validation_checks.py](../src/utils/validation_checks.py)). One naming quirk: `PurgedTimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)` actually yields **4** test folds, while the README calls it 5-fold and the submission README says 4-fold ([purged_cv.py](../src/data/purged_cv.py)).

The metrics follow from the class imbalance. If 70% of labels are FLAT, always predicting FLAT scores accuracy 0.70 and weighted-F1 ≈ 0.58, but macro-F1 ≈ 0.27 and MCC = 0 (illustrative arithmetic in the [validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md); see [Chicco & Jurman](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6941312/) on MCC). The project's synthetic Naive Flat lands almost exactly there (accuracy 0.698, macro-F1 0.274). That is why **macro-F1 is the headline metric**, and why learned models with balanced class weights show *lower* accuracy than Naive Flat ([exp A](../artifacts/experiment_a_results.csv)).

The whole network has about 60k parameters ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). If so, it is not a faithful dual-attention TLOB ([tlob.py](../src/models/tlob.py)). *Flag: this is a code reading from the research notes; the README does not mention it.*

### The execution simulator pays for every shortcut academic papers take

The simulator ([simulator.py](../src/execution/simulator.py); [strategies.py](../src/execution/strategies.py)) handles execution as follows. An aggressive order arrives at the first snapshot whose timestamp is ≥ t + latency. It walks up to 10 levels of that snapshot's book (an order larger than the visible book is extrapolated past the last level using the book's average level spacing and size, or filled partially or rejected, by a setting), pays the taker fee, and has its slippage measured against the decision-time mid. A passive order rests at the touch and fills only on a **trade-through**, meaning the best bid must trade *below* the order's price within 50 events. That is a conservative back-of-queue assumption, and it earns the maker rebate. The aggressive taker sends one lot per UP/DOWN signal up to a ±500-share inventory cap, with **no exit logic**. The passive maker keeps at most one working order and cancels when the signal flips, VPIN exceeds its train 90th percentile, or 50 events pass. An "academic" regime trades at the mid with no costs. That is the frictionless "fantasy" benchmark, matching DeepLOB's own simulation using "mid-prices without transaction costs" ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)).

These choices match the literature's fill hierarchy, which runs from optimistic touch-fill, through conservative trade-through, to explicit queue-position simulation ([Moallemi & Yuan](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25461/queue-value-2016.pdf); [Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)). The underlying cost arithmetic is simple. A taker pays about the half-spread plus the fee on every fill, so a round trip costs about the full spread plus twice the fee. For symmetric ±m moves with round-trip cost c, the breakeven hit rate is p* = (m + c)/(2m), which exceeds 1 whenever c > m (illustrative formula in the [execution notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md), see [Binance fee schedule](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/trading) for fee magnitudes). Synthetic parameters are $1M capital, 100-share lots, −0.5 bp maker / 1.5 bp taker, and 1 ms base latency. BTC uses 0.1 BTC lots, ±0.5 BTC, 0 bp maker / 4 bp taker ("Coinbase Pro fee schedule (April 2021), top volume tier"), and a latency grid in whole seconds ([common.py](../src/experiments/common.py); [README](../README.md)).

### Two C++ benchmarks: a stand-in ensemble, then the trained model

Two latency measurements exist, and you should keep them apart. Experiment D measures **single-sample inference of the trained models**: the LightGBM raw booster takes **0.138 ms** on CPU, and DeepLOB takes **2.365 ms** on CUDA at batch 1 (median across seeds) ([inference latency](../artifacts/experiment_d_inference_latency.csv)). The C++20 benchmark times feature extraction, tree inference and a simulated order on 100,000 random-walk snapshots, but its tree ensemble is **randomly generated** (50 trees × 3 classes, depth 6, random thresholds). It is not the trained LightGBM, and its C++ features are not checked against Python ([main_benchmark.cpp](../cpp/src/main_benchmark.cpp)). It reports **399k snapshots/s**, tree inference P50 1.8 µs, and end-to-end P99 6.3 µs. Commit `b4bafb7` separately claims a 2.41× speedup to **271 ns** per event from SoA + AVX2 + branchless trees, with no saved CSV ([C++ CSV](../artifacts/cpp_latency_benchmarks.csv)). The README is honest that these "are not the latency of the model evaluated above, and they do not include network or market-data parsing" ([README](../README.md)). Since then, the trained BTC LightGBM (327 trees, 20,601 leaves, 27 features) has been compiled to C++ by the project's own code generator ([lgbm_to_cpp.py](../scripts/lgbm_to_cpp.py)). It matches LightGBM exactly (maximum absolute score difference 0, 100% agreement on the predicted class) and takes **10.5 µs P50 / 17.9 µs P99** per row. That run shared the CPU with other jobs, so treat it as an upper bound ([trained-model CSV](../artifacts/cpp_trained_model_benchmark.csv)).

Every long run checkpoints at a natural boundary. The runner saves per session, FI-2010 per horizon, Optiver Close per training stage, and Optiver RVP per (fold, model) plus the recovered time order. `sync_checkpoints.py` mirrors `artifacts/` to an orphan `run-state` branch through a separate worktree, so runs can move between a laptop and Colab without committing Kaggle data to `master` ([sync_checkpoints.py](../scripts/sync_checkpoints.py); [colab notebook](../notebooks/colab_run_all.ipynb)). As a result `src/data/labels.py`, which every experiment imports through `common.py`, was absent from GitHub, and a fresh clone (including the Colab path) would fail with `ModuleNotFoundError` ([this_project notes, section 6](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/this_project.md)). **This has since been fixed.** The ignore rules were anchored to the repo root (`/data/`, `/logs/`), and `labels.py` was committed and pushed in **commit `6a6bcd5`**. ## Six datasets each test a different claim

Each dataset was chosen to test a different claim, so they should not be read as six repetitions of one experiment.

**Synthetic generator: a lab with a planted mechanism.** The generator has 10 levels and exponential inter-arrival times averaging 10 ms. Its event mix is 40% market orders, 40% limit orders and 20% cancellations. An informed burst starts at random (probability 0.08 in the large-tick regime) and lasts **15–59 events**. During a burst, market orders are 85/15 in the informed direction, limit insertions favour that side 70/30, and cancellations hit the opposite side ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)). Each seed produces 60,000 events (~10 simulated minutes), and results average 5 seeds ([README](../README.md)). Because the mechanism is known, the generator can show whether models find it. Results therefore describe the generator, not markets. Note also that the docstring's claim of "Hawkes self-exciting order arrival" does not match the code, which uses i.i.d. exponential arrivals, and that a `noise_volatility` parameter is defined but never used.

**Coinbase BTC-USD: real fees on real flow.** This is the Kaggle "High Frequency Crypto Limit Order Book Data" set (martinsn, CC0): **1,030,728** one-second rows from 7 to 19 April 2021, with 15 levels per side, of which the loader uses 10. Prices are rebuilt from distance-to-mid columns. A trade tape with true aggressor side is reconstructed from per-second buy and sell notional ([crypto_loader.py](../src/data/crypto_loader.py)). The data is cut into **4 contiguous segments of 257,682 rows** (~3 days each), each with its own 70/15/15 split. k is measured in 1-second rows, so k=20 means 20 seconds ([common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)).

**FI-2010: the published-benchmark sanity check.** FI-2010 covers five Finnish stocks on Nasdaq Nordic over 10 days in June 2010, one row per 10 events, with 144 features (40 raw LOB + 104 handcrafted) ([Ntakaris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03233)). The project uses **Setup 2**: train on days 1–7 and test on days 8–10, the same files DeepLOB's public code uses. It uses **no class weights**, and its headline F1 is support-weighted, to match DeepLOB's tables ([fi2010_benchmark.py](../src/experiments/fi2010_benchmark.py)). Using weighted F1 is deliberate. In DeepLOB's tables recall equals accuracy in every row, which is the mathematical signature of support-weighted averaging ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). FI-2010 has known problems: it is small, old, pre-normalized, and labelled with a threshold chosen for class balance ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)).

**Optiver Realized Volatility (2021): a leakage case study.** The task is to predict next-10-minute RV per (stock_id, time_id) from 10 minutes of 2-level book and trade data. time_ids were shuffled, and the host said a shuffled time_id "will not contain any information" ([host FAQ](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/249752)). The 1st-place solution (nyanp) recovered real prices as 0.01 / (smallest normalized tick), then recovered **time order** with a 1-D t-SNE of the time_id × stock price matrix. It then built nearest-neighbour features from similar time_ids, which took the score "from 0.21 to 0.19" ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). The project copies the order recovery, runs **forward expanding-window CV** (4 folds of 10%) on the recovered order, and compares three versions of the neighbour features: none, past-only, and transductive (the winner's version, which reaches into the future). It adds a shuffled K-fold run for contrast ([optiver_rvp.py](../src/experiments/optiver_rvp.py)).

**Optiver Trading at the Close (2023): online learning under drift.** The task is to predict each stock's 60-second WAP move relative to a synthetic Nasdaq index, in basis points, during the 10-minute closing auction, scored by MAE. The data covers 200 stocks, 55 snapshots per day and 481 training days ([data page](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/data); [index weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851)). The project follows the 1st-place split: train on date_id < 400 and hold out the last 81 days. It builds 93 features, including the winner's "magic" seconds-bucket-group and cross-sectional ratio and rank features plus the previous day's revealed target. It compares a zero baseline, a static LightGBM (MAE objective, 255 leaves), and the same model **retrained every 12 holdout days**. Post-processing subtracts the cross-sectional mean prediction, with equal weights because the index weights are not in `train.csv` ([optiver_close.py](../src/experiments/optiver_close.py)).

**LOBSTER: Nasdaq event data for the tick-size question.** The free LOBSTER sample files hold every order event (10 levels) for INTC, MSFT, AAPL, AMZN and GOOG on 21 June 2012, 9:30–16:00. The project resamples them onto a 100 ms clock, which makes horizons from 100 ms to 30 s possible. The stocks split cleanly into large-tick (INTC and MSFT: the tick is 3.3–3.7 bp of the price and the spread sits at one tick 77% of the time) and small-tick (AAPL, AMZN and GOOG: 0.17–0.45 bp). Every execution is recorded, so passive fills by queue position can be simulated almost exactly. The files are for academic use and are not redistributed ([lobster_loader.py](../src/data/lobster_loader.py); [exp_i_lobster.py](../src/experiments/exp_i_lobster.py)).

Horizons are **not like-for-like across datasets**. A synthetic event is ~10 ms (k=20 ≈ 200 ms), a BTC row is 1 s (k=20 = 20 s), an FI-2010 row is 10 events, and a LOBSTER row is 100 ms. Never put the synthetic, BTC and FI-2010 "k=20" numbers on one axis as if they were the same horizon.

## Ten audit bugs had manufactured the original headline

The first version was built on 23–24 October 2026. Its README claimed "a fast C++ decision tree ensemble (LightGBM) achieves higher realized net Sharpe ratios than mathematically superior but computationally slower deep learning models" ([pre-audit README via `git show 7767a7d^`](../README.md)). An audit (artifacts regenerated 26 October, committed as `7767a7d` on 30 October) found that most of the evidence for that claim was produced by bugs. If you present this project, this table is the most instructive part, because each row is a pitfall that published work has also fallen into.

| # | Bug | Symptom | Fix | Guarding test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Momentum baseline used y[t−1], which already contains k−1 future events | Momentum macro-F1 **0.9646**, far above LightGBM 0.6039 | Predict y[t−k], the last *realized* label → 0.519 | `test_momentum_uses_only_realized_labels` |
| 2 | "DeepLOB" latency curve was LightGBM signals rerun with +4 ticks of delay | Fabricated speed-vs-accuracy chart | Exp A returns real DeepLOB predictions; Exp D executes them; inference measured | — |
| 3 | VPIN without timestamps was `clip(|imbalance_l1|·0.5 + 0.3)` | "VPIN" was a copy of L1 imbalance | Generator emits timestamps; real volume-clock VPIN; NaN if no tape | `test_pipeline_uses_real_vpin_and_tick_spread` |

| 10 | Latency counted in events, not time | Latency had no physical meaning | `searchsorted(timestamps, t + latency_ms)` | `test_latency_uses_timestamps` |

Sources: diffs and pre-audit artifacts in commit `7767a7d` as summarized in [this_project notes, section 2](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/this_project.md); [baselines.py](../src/models/baselines.py); [simulator.py](../src/execution/simulator.py); [metrics.py](../src/execution/metrics.py); [tests](../tests). The test count rose from 12 to 24. *Flag: a "−0.999 correlation" between rel_spread and price level was quoted in an earlier brief, but the audit script's output was not saved, so that figure cannot be verified from files.* The **eleventh** issue, found later, was the untracked `labels.py` described above, now fixed in `6a6bcd5`.

Three of these bugs are worth explaining carefully, because they generalize. **Bug 1 is label overlap in disguise.** With k-step smoothed labels, the label at t−1 shares almost all of its future window with the label at t, so "repeat the previous label" is a near-copy of the answer. Lee (2024) found the same effect in published work: a naive lagged-target forecast reached 0.648 accuracy against DeepLOB's 0.659 ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14157v1)). **Bug 4 is the classic "illegitimate feature".** The model memorized the price level through a float rounding artifact. It looked like microstructure skill and was nothing of the kind. **Bug 5 is the Sharpe trap.** Lo (2002) shows that serial correlation alone can overstate annualized Sharpe by up to 65% ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2002/the-statistics-of-sharpe-ratios)). Annualizing 90 seconds of event-level P&L multiplies a noisy estimate by an enormous √(periods per year), which is why the README now says such numbers "mean nothing" ([README](../README.md)). After the fix the synthetic CSVs still contain 100 ms-bar annualized Sharpes (e.g. 636.5 ± 359.7). Do not quote them.

The net effect reversed the project's story. Without the leaky baseline, learned models add about **0.04–0.07 macro-F1** over an honest naive rule rather than losing to it. Without the fake curve, LightGBM's profit advantage over DeepLOB appears at **zero latency**, so it comes from better signals, not from speed.

Source: [fi2010_setup2_summary.csv](../artifacts/fi2010/fi2010_setup2_summary.csv), means over 3 training seeds (logistic regression is deterministic); published rows from [DeepLOB Table II](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668), which gives no Setup-2 number at k=100. *History* adds lagged changes of the 40 raw book columns to the 144 handcrafted features, so the tabular models see the past that DeepLOB sees in its window. The project's DeepLOB is within **1.4 / 0.8 / 2.3 accuracy points** and **1.2 / 0.3 / 2.3 weighted-F1 points** of the paper at k = 10 / 20 / 50, and the seed-to-seed spread is at most 0.5 points. That is strong evidence the implementation is sound, and it rules out "your DeepLOB is broken" as the explanation for its weak showing on BTC. The shape across horizons matches the literature as well. Accuracy dips at k=20 and recovers at k=50/100, as in DeepLOB's own table (84.47 / 74.85 / 80.51) and in TLOB's re-run of DeepLOB (F1 71.1 / 62.4 / 75.4 / 77.6) ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). That is the opposite of the monotone decay seen on BTC.

An earlier version of this report listed three competing explanations for DeepLOB's large lead (0.28–0.40 macro-F1) over the single-row tabular models: unmatched inputs, smoothed labels, and distribution shift. Two have since been tested. **Inputs explain the short horizons.** With history features, logistic regression beats DeepLOB at k = 10 (86.1% vs 83.1%) and k = 20 (78.3% vs 74.1%), gaps several times the seed spread. DeepLOB's short-horizon advantage came from seeing the past, not from its architecture. At k = 50 and k = 100 DeepLOB still wins clearly (by 12 and 1.8 points over the best tabular model), so at longer horizons the architecture does add something. **Distribution shift explains LightGBM's failure.** LightGBM with history collapses at k = 20 (56.6%). A diagnostic ([diagnose_fi2010_lightgbm.py](../scripts/diagnose_fi2010_lightgbm.py)) shows it is not an early-stopping problem: test accuracy is 54–57% at every number of boosting rounds, while validation accuracy sits at 86–88% for every setting. The test days are calmer than the training days (FLAT is 62% of test labels against 49% of training labels at k = 20). LightGBM learns to call moves from the size of recent book changes and, on calm days, predicts far too few FLATs, while logistic regression degrades much less. The smoothed-label explanation (Lee 2024) is still untested: re-labelling FI-2010 with a non-smoothed target remains open. So "simple beats deep" is **horizon- and input-dependent, not universal**.

## The project lands where careful literature lands, not where hype does

**DeepLOB and FI-2010.** The project's DeepLOB accuracies (83.1 / 74.1 / 78.2% at k = 10 / 20 / 50, 3 seeds) sit much closer to DeepLOB's published 84.47 / 74.85 / 80.51% than to independent re-runs. TLOB re-ran DeepLOB at **71.1** F1 at h=10, and LOBCAST measured **71.4 ± 5.3** average F1 on FI-2010 against a claimed 78.9 ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757); [LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). One plausible explanation is the metric. The project matches DeepLOB's support-weighted F1 and no-class-weight setup, while TLOB and LOBCAST do not state their averaging ([deep-learning notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/deep_learning_lob_benchmarks.md)). *This explanation is an inference, not a verified finding.* The project's own macro-F1 at k=10 is 0.72 against a weighted-F1 of 0.82, which shows how much weighted-F1 flatters results when FLAT is 71% of the test set. At k=100, where classes are nearly balanced, the two metrics converge (0.775 vs 0.776). The more important literature result is that FI-2010 scores do not transfer. On 2021–2022 Nasdaq data every model in LOBCAST fell to **48–61% F1**, and plain MLP and CNN models came within a few points of the best ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). On FI-2010, scores *rise* with horizon, the opposite of real NASDAQ and BTC data ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The project reproduces both patterns. Its FI-2010 DeepLOB is *better* at k=50/100 than at k=20, while its BTC models decay monotonically from 10 s to 100 s. Treat the FI-2010 result as a check that the implementation is sound, not as evidence about markets.

**Trees versus deep nets.** No verified peer-reviewed LOB benchmark pits LightGBM against DeepLOB/TLOB on identical splits ([deep-learning notes, section 9](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/deep_learning_lob_benchmarks.md)). The project's synthetic and BTC rankings are therefore a small contribution in themselves. On real BTC, trees ≥ TLOB > momentum > DeepLOB, with LightGBM beating DeepLOB in 4/4 segments. This is consistent with Grinsztajn et al.'s finding that tree models remain state of the art on medium-sized tabular data ([arXiv 2207.08815](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)). It is also consistent with LOBCAST's observation that on new data simple models come within a few F1 points of the best deep ones ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)), and with the Optiver RVP winner's own ablation. There, a **single LightGBM** scored 0.19699 private and would have won alone, with the conclusion "feature diversity was more important than model diversity" ([ablation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/302626)). FI-2010 is only a partial counter-example: with matched history a linear model beats DeepLOB at short horizons, and DeepLOB wins only at k = 50–100. So the honest summary is "**trees on engineered features match or beat deep nets on real BTC (significantly: +0.058 macro-F1 over DeepLOB, block bootstrap); on FI-2010 deep sequence models win at longer horizons and simple models with history win at short ones**". It is not "trees win".

**BTC against TLOB's BTC results.** TLOB reports Binance BTCUSDT F1 falling from **74.7 at ~1 s to 41.49 at ~10 s**, with DeepLOB at 68.07 → 37.43 ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The project's LightGBM at 10 s scores 0.540 macro-F1 on Coinbase (DeepLOB at 20 s: 0.401). That looks better than TLOB at 10 s, but **the numbers are not comparable**. The exchange (Coinbase spot vs Binance perpetual), year (2021 vs 2023), sampling (1 s vs 250 ms), label threshold (train 1/3-quantile vs TLOB's θ) and F1 averaging all differ. What does transfer is the shape: steep decay with horizon, and small gaps between architectures. TLOB itself tested a cost-aware threshold only on TSLA (F1 falling to 41.39 / 36.48 / 30.82). It could not apply it to Intel, where 99.99% of labels became "stable", and it ran no profit backtest ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The BTC experiment supplies exactly that missing step, and finds the edge four to five times smaller than the fee.

**Predictability versus profitability.** This is the project's main thesis, and it matches the best papers. Lucchese, Pakkanen and Veraart find predictability "ubiquitous" but lasting only **50–300 book updates**, measured on mid-to-mid returns that "are not tradable in practice" ([arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777)). Kolm et al. put the effective horizon at about **two average price changes** ([Kolm et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v33y2023i4p1044-1081.html)). Briola, Bartolucci and Aste conclude that "traditional machine learning metrics fail to adequately assess the quality of forecasts" in LOBs, and they find DeepLOB near chance on small-tick stocks (MCC **0.01–0.04** at 50–100 updates) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/)). LOBCAST's trading simulation found profitability "far from guaranteed" ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). The project adds a quantitative bridge between the two literatures, showing where on the cost axis the edge disappears.

**Optiver RVP against the leaderboard.** The project's numbers sit on a different scale from the leaderboard and should not be ranked against it. They come from forward CV on training data with an early-stopping bias, while Kaggle scored a private, later period. Directionally, though, they line up. The project's naive 0.337 matches Optiver's 0.341 ([tutorial](https://www.kaggle.com/code/jiashenliu/introduction-to-financial-concepts-and-data)). Its base LightGBM at 0.233 is near the winner's no-neighbour LightGBM at **0.22492** (rank #1358). Its leaky transductive 0.213 points toward the winner's **0.19548**. The winner's own ablation shows that "tick-size leak-free" volatility and trade-size neighbours alone reach **0.20367**, while real-price neighbours push the score to #1 ([ablation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/302626)). nyanp himself wrote, "I don't think we can use future information in a real Optiver's scenario" ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). The hosts briefly added noise to weaken the trick, which crashed many top teams (nyanp went from 2nd to 3836th), and then reverted it ([noise update](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/282791); [revert](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/284121)). The project's contribution here is to measure how much of the leaderboard gap is future leakage, on the winner's own pipeline.

**Optiver Close against the winner.** The 1st-place solution (hyd) blended **CatBoost 0.5 / GRU 0.3 / Transformer 0.2** on 300 features. It trained on the first 400 days with the last 81 held out, **retrained every 12 days** during the forecasting phase, and subtracted the index-weighted mean prediction. It scored **5.4030** private MAE ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/487446)). In the winner's ablation, online learning moved the blend from **5.4438 to 5.4030**, about 0.04 MAE. That exceeds the gap between 1st and 10th place (5.4030 vs 5.4379) ([leaderboard](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/leaderboard)). The all-zeros submission scored **5.465** on the public period ([Pims](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/443195)), so even the winner improved on zero by only about 1.1%. That comparison is approximate because the public and private periods differ. The project's absolute MAE (5.8476) is **not comparable** to the leaderboard. Its holdout is days 400–480 of the training file, a different and apparently noisier period than Kaggle's private test (its zero baseline is 5.9936, not ~5.465). The *relative* gain is comparable in size, though: **2.4% over zero** for the project versus about 1.1% for the winner, measured on different periods. Both say the same thing, that closing-auction moves are almost unpredictable and that skill is measured in fractions of a basis point. The clear gap is retraining. The project's 12-day refits gained ~0.1% where the winner's gained ~0.75%. The project differs from the winner in three known ways: LightGBM only (no GRU/Transformer, about 93 rather than 300 features), equal-weighted rather than index-weighted demeaning, and its holdout period. The index weights are recoverable by regression with R² ≈ 0.99999999 ([weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851)).

## Limitations and pitfalls to state before anyone asks

The README keeps a list of limitations ([README](../README.md)). The real-data evidence is still narrow: one crypto pair over 12 days in April 2021, one 10-day 2010 equity benchmark, one day of Nasdaq event data for five stocks, and two Kaggle datasets. Synthetic test windows are ~90 s, so PnL is noisy. The four BTC segments give only four paired observations per comparison, so "4/4 segments" is suggestive rather than statistically strong: a sign test on 4/4 has p = 0.0625 one-sided. Experiment J addresses this with a moving-block bootstrap and McNemar's test on the pooled test rows, which make LightGBM's lead over every other model significant, though over Random Forest by only +0.004. Experiment D's strategy is one lot per signal with no confidence sizing and no exit logic; Experiment E adds confidence filtering and fixed-hold round trips. The book does not react to the project's own orders. Deep models get ≤10 epochs with little tuning (8 on BTC, ≤15 on FI-2010). The main C++ benchmark runs a random ensemble; the trained model has its own benchmark, but it was run on a busy machine. On FI-2010 the tabular and deep models see different inputs, one row against a 100-row window, so that ranking is not an apples-to-apples comparison. The Optiver Close holdout is a slice of the training file, not Kaggle's private period. To these, add the issues the research notes found in code. TLOB attends only over time, if the code reading is right. GPU nondeterminism moves DeepLOB results. The generator's docstring over-claims Hawkes arrivals. The BTC "1 ms" base latency actually means "the next 1-second snapshot". FI-2010 test windows can cross stock boundaries, which is inherited from the benchmark files. `src/config.py` holds vestigial parameters that nothing uses ([this_project notes, section 7](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/this_project.md)).

Several deeper pitfalls belong in any talk. **Replay cannot capture your own impact.** If a simulated market order consumes the ask, the next replayed snapshot still shows the historical ask, as if you had never traded. Replay P&L is an upper bound that is valid only for orders much smaller than top-of-book depth ([JAX-LOB](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.13289); [Tóth et al. on square-root impact](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694v3)). **One-second snapshots hide sub-second structure**: which side traded first, quote flickers, and the latency race that Budish, Cramton and Shim show is won by the fastest firm ([BCS](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69665.pdf)). **Effective sample size is far smaller than row count.** With horizon-h overlapping labels, N_eff ≈ N/h. *(Flag: this is a standard time-series approximation stated from memory in the [validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md).)* So 38,633 BTC test rows at k=20 behave like roughly 2,000 independent observations, and per-seed standard deviations understate uncertainty. **Multiple testing** applies too. Every horizon, threshold, feature set and architecture tried counts as a trial. With many trials, the best backtest is expected to look good by chance. That motivates the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting ([Bailey & López de Prado, DSR](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551); [PBO](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)), and Harvey, Liu and Zhu's t > 3 hurdle ([NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)). **The permutation test only catches misalignment.** As the project now says honestly, it "cannot prove that features don't look ahead" ([README](../README.md)). **VPIN may be proxying activity.** Andersen and Bondarenko's critique implies VPIN's 7.5–9.7% gain share (synthetic and BTC) should be checked against volume and trade-count features ([A&B](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf)). **Displayed imbalance can be spoofed**, a live-trading risk that offline datasets cannot reveal ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=27752)). **"Shuffled" data is rarely shuffled.** Optiver's time_ids were reordered within weeks ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). Finally, fee schedules are a moving target. The project's 4 bp BTC taker fee is the April 2021 top tier. Current retail schedules are far higher (Binance Regular 10 bp taker), and current Coinbase tiers are *unverified*, with secondary sources in conflict ([Binance](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/trading); [Altrady](https://www.altrady.com/blog/crypto-trading-platforms/coinbase-advanced-trade)). The BTC loss is therefore, if anything, optimistic for a retail trader.

**P0: make the finished numbers defensible.** With all runs complete, the four-segment BTC means and the full FI-2010 table exist, but only as means, standard deviations and win counts. For every model pair, report per-segment and per-day *paired* differences with a Wilcoxon or paired t-test, and a block bootstrap with block length ≥ k. These are the protocols recommended in the validation notes. Henderson et al. and Bouthillier et al. are cited there for seed variance (*flag: those two references were not fetched*) ([validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md)). Fix RVP by carving an inner validation slice from each training window for early stopping, so the scored fold is untouched. Rerun the shuffled K-fold with the same feature set as the forward variants, to remove the `stock_rv_target_ratio` confound. Keep a running count of every configuration tried, so a Deflated-Sharpe-style correction is possible later ([DSR](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)).

**P1: trade only when confident.** The BTC taker loses because it acts on all ~15,600 signals per segment, each with ~$0.52 of edge against ~$2.41 of fee. To break even before slippage, a selected subset needs roughly 4.6× the average edge per trade. The cheapest test is an **accuracy-coverage and PnL-coverage curve**. Rank test rows by max class probability, then for coverage levels {100%, 50%, 20%, 10%, 5%, 2%, 1%} run the existing simulator only on that top slice. Plot net PnL per trade against coverage, with a day-level bootstrap CI. Add calibration first: isotonic regression for LightGBM and temperature scaling for DeepLOB, fitted on the time-ordered validation split, with reliability diagrams and ECE. Then wrap predictions in **adaptive conformal inference**, α_{t+1} = α_t + γ(α − err_t), with γ ∈ {0.005, 0.01, 0.05}, and trade only when the prediction set is a singleton UP or DOWN ([Gibbs & Candès](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170); [Zaffran et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07282)). *Flag: these conformal references are background knowledge, not re-fetched.* The success criterion is concrete. A positive net PnL per trade at some coverage level, with a bootstrap CI excluding zero across all four segments, would be the project's first evidence of a tradable edge. A null result is equally publishable. Add exit logic (hold for k rows, then flatten) at the same time, because the current strategy never closes positions.

**P2: change the target to what a trader is paid for.** Replace "smoothed mid moved more than θ" with a **tradable label**. UP if the best bid at t+h minus the current best ask exceeds the round-trip fee; DOWN symmetrically; FLAT otherwise. Report the class balance per dataset (expect FLAT to dominate on BTC, as TLOB found for Intel) and evaluate by simulator PnL and hit rate on trades taken, not F1 ([new-directions notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/new_directions.md); [TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Two variants follow. The first is a **TLOB-label ablation**: decouple the smoothing window k from horizon h (k ∈ {1, 5, 10, h} at fixed h) and show whether F1 and PnL move together. TLOB did not test profitability, so this fills a documented gap ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The second is **triple-barrier labels with meta-labelling**, where a second model decides whether to act on LightGBM's signal ([López de Prado, AFML](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086); *flag: background reference*). Triple-barrier labels overlap, so keep purging.

**P3: give LightGBM a fair fight on FI-2010.** The FI-2010 ranking is the result most likely to be misquoted, so isolate its causes. (a) Give LightGBM and LR the same history DeepLOB sees: lags 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 of the 40 LOB columns plus rolling means and standard deviations, or a 100-row window of compressed features (OFI, imbalance, mid returns) computed from the raw columns. (b) Re-label FI-2010 from its mid-prices with a *non-smoothed* target, either the mid at t+k versus the mid at t or TLOB's decoupled-window label, and rerun both model families. Lee (2024) predicts that the deep model's advantage shrinks on non-smoothed targets ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14157v1)). (c) Report per-day results for days 8, 9 and 10 to show how much of LightGBM's below-flat accuracy comes from distribution shift. If LightGBM with history closes most of the 0.3 macro-F1 gap, the project's story becomes unified: inputs and labels, not architecture, decide the ranking.

**P4: turn the Optiver Close result into a proper ablation.** The static (5.8564), demeaned (5.8533) and retrained (5.8476) MAEs exist. Add three things. (a) Retrain schedules of 0, 1 and 6 updates, mirroring the winner's 0/1/5-update table, where the blend went 5.4438 → 5.4157 → 5.4030 ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/487446)). (b) **Recovered index weights**: regress the implied index return on the 200 stock returns over training days, then use index-weighted rather than equal-weighted demeaning, as the 1st and 9th place did ([weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851); [9th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/486868)). (c) A Diebold–Mariano test or day-level block bootstrap on daily MAE differences, because the whole leaderboard spread is a few hundredths of a basis point. An optional fourth step is LightGBM warm-start (`init_model`) and `refit` with decay, both used by the 5th-place team ([5th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/462743)).

**P5: give the deep models a fair fight.** Rewrite TLOB so one attention block runs over the feature axis and one over time, per the paper. Add MLPLOB, which roughly ties TLOB on FI-2010 (81.64 vs 81.55 at h=10), suggesting attention is not the key ingredient ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Add BiN-CTABL, the most robust model in LOBCAST at 61.2 F1 on LOB-2021 ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). Add a **one-feature queue-imbalance logistic regression** as a literature-grounded baseline that any deep model must beat ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Train deep models for 30–50 epochs with patience ≥ 5 (BTC DeepLOB got 8) and fix seeds, and report whether the synthetic and BTC rankings survive. DeepLOB's large BTC segment spread (0.333 to 0.442 macro-F1) suggests unstable training rather than a stable ceiling. If LightGBM still wins, the "trees win on engineered features" claim gets much stronger. If not, the project learns something important.

**P6: benchmark the model you actually trained.** Save the BTC LightGBM `model.txt`. Compile it with **TL2cgen** (C99 code, built with `-O3 -march=native` into the existing C++ harness) and **lleaves** (LLVM), and compare against the LightGBM C API single-row path and ONNX Runtime with one thread. Measure p50/p99/p99.9 at batch 1 over 10⁵–10⁶ *real* test feature rows, check that the backends agree numerically (max abs diff), and port the Python feature computation to C++ with a parity test. The vendor benchmark shows lleaves at 9.61 µs vs LightGBM 52.31 µs at batch 1 on old hardware ([lleaves](https://siboehm.com/articles/21/lleaves); [TL2cgen docs](https://tl2cgen.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html)). Even so, the project's own latency sweep shows that microseconds do not matter for a 1-second-snapshot strategy. Frame this as engineering hygiene, not as alpha.

**P7: interrogate the features.** Add raw volume and trade-count features, then ablate VPIN to test whether its 9.7% gain share on BTC survives once trading intensity is available. Andersen and Bondarenko predict it largely will not ([A&B](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf)). Replace split-gain attribution with **TreeSHAP** (`pred_contrib=True`), grouping correlated OFI levels before interpreting, and compare attributions across horizons ([Lundberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04610); *flag: background reference*).

**P8: replicate two literature results on data you own.** First, a **multi-horizon regression** in the style of Kolm et al.: predict log mid-returns at h ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100} rows, comparing OFI inputs against raw-book inputs. Report out-of-sample R² and Spearman IC per horizon, and expect tiny R² with a peak at short horizons ([Kolm et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3900141)). Second, **BTC→ETH cross-OFI**. The Kaggle crypto dataset reportedly also holds ETH and ADA (*flag: confirmed only via a third-party mirror; same timestamps and sampling not confirmed*) ([mirror](https://baselight.app/u/kaggle/dataset/martinsn_high_frequency_crypto_limit_order_book_data)). Add lagged BTC integrated OFI at 1, 2, 5 and 10 s to an ETH model and test CCZ's pattern: contemporaneous cross-impact adds nothing, lagged cross-impact helps briefly ([CCZ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)).

**P9: treat non-stationarity as an experimental variable.** Using the four BTC segments as a natural time series, compare four set-ups: a static model trained on segment 0 and tested on segments 1–3; expanding-window retraining; sliding-window retraining; and LightGBM warm-start updates. Report per-day metrics as a time series rather than one pooled number. Optionally add filtered (never smoothed) HMM regime probabilities from rolling volatility, spread and OFI variance ([new-directions notes, section 6](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/new_directions.md)). This carries the Optiver Close lesson over to crypto.

**Stretch: make passive strategies credible.** The passive maker is the strategy that could plausibly beat fees, and it is also the one the current data can model least well. Record one to two weeks of Coinbase `level2` and `matches` WebSocket data, or download a free event-level day from tardis.dev (*flag: free-day policy unconfirmed*) ([tardis docs](https://docs.tardis.dev/api)). Fit a **queue-reactive model**, in which order-arrival rates depend on queue sizes, to simulate passive fills by queue position rather than by trade-through ([Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)). On top of that, test **Avellaneda–Stoikov market making** with the reservation price r = s − q·γ·σ²·(T − t), shifted by the model's forecast ([Avellaneda & Stoikov](https://www.math.nyu.edu/~avellane/HighFrequencyTrading.pdf); *flag: background reference*). Report PnL, inventory variance, fill rate and post-fill adverse selection. Deep RL execution, generative simulators (TRADES/DeepMarket, LOBS5) and time-series foundation models are further out. For the last of these, the one verified finance study found zero-shot Chronos at **−1.37%** out-of-sample R² with directional accuracy just above 51% ([Rahimikia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18578v1); *flag: verified via search snippet only*), so treat it as a cheap negative-result baseline at most.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Limit order book (LOB) | The exchange's list of resting buy (bid) and sell (ask) orders at each price |
| Level / depth | The k-th best price on a side / the total size resting there |
| Tick size | The minimum price increment |
| Large-tick vs small-tick | Spread almost always one tick (queues decide moves) vs several ticks (moves happen inside the spread) |
| Spread / mid-price | Best ask − best bid / their average |
| Queue imbalance | (bid size − ask size)/(bid size + ask size) at the best quotes |
| Weighted mid / WAP | Best prices weighted by the *opposite* side's size; Optiver's price proxy |
| Micro-price | Expected long-run mid given imbalance and spread (Stoikov) |
| OFI / MLOFI / integrated OFI | Net change in best-quote supply and demand / the same per level / first principal component across levels |
| VPIN | Average buy–sell volume imbalance over equal-volume buckets; a "toxicity" gauge |
| Volume clock | Measuring time in traded volume rather than seconds |
| Adverse selection / toxic flow | Losing to better-informed counterparties; flow that does this to market makers |
| Taker / maker | Crosses the spread with a market order and pays a fee / rests a limit order and may earn a rebate |
| Book walking | A large market order consuming several price levels |
| Trade-through fill | A passive order counts as filled only once the price trades beyond it |
| Slippage | Execution price minus the decision-time mid |
| Fantasy / academic regime | Trading at the mid with no costs; an upper bound |
| Horizon k | How far ahead the label looks: events (synthetic), 1 s rows (BTC), 10-event rows (FI-2010) |
| Smoothed label / FLAT band α | Label from an averaged future mid; moves smaller than α count as FLAT |
| Leakage | Information unavailable at prediction time reaching the model |
| Transductive features | Features computed using the test set or future rows (e.g. RVP neighbours) |
| Purging / embargo | Dropping training rows whose labels overlap test labels / an extra buffer after the test fold |
| Macro-F1 / weighted-F1 | Unweighted mean of per-class F1 / support-weighted mean (tracks the majority class) |
| MCC / Cohen's kappa | Chance-corrected agreement scores; 0 for constant or random predictions |
| RMSPE / MAE | Root-mean-square *percentage* error (RVP) / mean absolute error (Close) |
| Realized volatility | √(Σ squared log returns) over a window |
| Setup 2 (FI-2010) | Train on days 1–7, test on days 8–10 |
| DeepLOB / TLOB / BiN | CNN-Inception-LSTM LOB model / dual-attention transformer / bilinear input normalization |
| LightGBM | Gradient-boosted decision trees |
| Online learning | Periodically retraining on newly revealed data |
| PBO / Deflated Sharpe | Probability that the in-sample winner underperforms out of sample / Sharpe adjusted for number of trials |
| Effective sample size | Number of truly independent observations, roughly N/h for overlapping h-step labels |

## Conclusion

The project's real contribution is not a model. It is a **measuring instrument**: one pipeline in which the same test rows, the same purges and the same cost model apply to every predictor, so gaps between models and between "predictable" and "profitable" can be read directly. With the runs finished, the instrument gives a consistent reading across six very different datasets. Predictability is real but small and short-lived: about +0.05 macro-F1 over a naive rule at 20 s on BTC, and none left by 100 s. A 2.4% MAE edge over zero in the closing auction is the same order as the Kaggle winners'. Model rankings depend on inputs and labels more than on architecture. Trees on engineered features match or beat every deep net on real BTC, while on FI-2010 DeepLOB wins at long horizons and a linear model with history wins at short ones. Leakage moves results by amounts comparable to genuine modelling gains: on Optiver RVP, future-peeking neighbours (0.009 RMSPE) and shuffled CV (0.021) are each about as large as, or larger than, the legitimate gain from past-only neighbours (0.011). The audit is the sharpest version of that lesson. The leaky baseline and the fabricated latency curve are strengths of the write-up, because they show concretely how easily LOB results are manufactured.

The follow-up experiments then tested the escape routes this report used to recommend. Trading only the most confident signals raises the gross edge to about 0.5–0.7 bp per round trip, and cost-aware labels add little. No volatility, spread or VPIN regime survives costs. Rebate-earning quoting fails on BTC because there is no spread to earn, and on large-tick stocks its result depends on queue position, which is a speed race. Splitting the round trip shows why modelling alone cannot close the fee gap: on the most confident rows, even a perfect direction forecast earns about 4.3 bp, below the 8 bp taker round trip. The negative result is now rigorous. The constructive lesson, set out in the [final report](../submission/PROJECT_REPORT.md), is a balancing rule. Costs decide whether a profit is possible at all, accuracy decides whether you clear the bar, and latency matters in proportion to how fast the signal decays, which is fastest for the best signals.
