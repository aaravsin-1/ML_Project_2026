# Order-book signals survive the audit, not the fees

This project (repository `ML_Project_2026`) asks two linked questions. First, can the direction of the next few hundred milliseconds to tens of seconds of price movement be predicted from the state of a limit order book (LOB)? Second, does that prediction still make money once you pay the spread, exchange fees and latency? So far the answer is **yes, it can be predicted, and mostly no, it does not make money**. On the synthetic market, where the results are final over 5 seeds, LightGBM on 27 microstructure features reaches **macro-F1 0.584 ± 0.012**, against **0.519** for an honest "repeat the last realized label" rule. Crossing the spread leaves **24.6%** of the frictionless profit for LightGBM and **5.8%** for DeepLOB ([exp A](../artifacts/experiment_a_results.csv); [exp D](../artifacts/experiment_d_execution_strategies.csv)). On real Coinbase BTC data (partial: 1 of 4 segments complete) the learned model again beats the naive baselines (macro-F1 **0.488 vs 0.436**). However, a 4 bp taker fee turns **+$7,707 of frictionless profit into −$36,831**, because the fee (~$2.40 per trade) is about five times the per-trade edge (~$0.48) ([BTC log](../logs/btc_run.log)). The other real-data checks are mixed. On FI-2010 the project's own DeepLOB comes within **1.6 accuracy points** of the published k=10 number (partial: other horizons still training) ([FI-2010 k=10](../artifacts/fi2010/setup2_k10_e15.csv)). On Optiver Realized Volatility (final), the 1st-place solution's "neighbour" features earn about 0.009 RMSPE of their improvement from future information ([RVP summary](../artifacts/optiver_rvp/rvp_summary.csv)). Optiver Trading at the Close is still running. Before any of this, an audit found ten bugs that had produced a fake headline: a momentum baseline that peeked at the future (macro-F1 0.965) and a "DeepLOB" latency curve that was really LightGBM run twice. Fixing them reversed the thesis from "speed beats model complexity" to "**signal quality beats speed, and fees beat both**" ([commit 7767a7d](../README.md)). That conclusion matches the most careful recent literature, which finds that LOB predictability is real but short-lived and rarely tradable after costs ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915); [Briola et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/); [TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Training runs on BTC segments 1–3, the FI-2010 horizons k=20/50/100 and Optiver Close were still running when this report was written; their final numbers will be added when they finish.

## How to explain this in two minutes

If you have to explain the project out loud, this script follows the logic in the order a listener needs it. "Every exchange keeps a list of people waiting to buy and sell at each price. That list is the limit order book. When the queue of sellers at the best price is much shorter than the queue of buyers, the next price move is more likely to be up, because only a few shares need to trade or cancel to clear the sellers. Researchers have shown this empirically for Nasdaq stocks ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)), and DeepLOB-style neural networks try to learn it automatically ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). My project builds one pipeline that turns order-book snapshots into features such as order-flow imbalance, queue imbalance and the micro-price. It trains a ladder of models from naive rules up to DeepLOB and a transformer, all scored on exactly the same test rows. Then it pushes their buy and sell signals through an execution simulator that pays the spread, the exchange fee and a realistic delay. The first version of my project looked spectacular, but an audit showed that its best baseline was secretly reading the future, and its 'fast model beats slow model' chart was a fabrication. After fixing ten such bugs, the honest story is this. The models do predict direction better than chance and better than a naive rule. That edge mostly comes from the shape of the top three book levels, and it is small. On real Bitcoin data the edge per trade is about a fifth of the trading fee, so a strategy that trades on every signal loses heavily. Speed matters far less than signal quality: DeepLOB's extra 2 milliseconds of thinking cost about $10, while its weaker signals cost about $350. I also used two Optiver Kaggle competitions to show how leakage inflates leaderboard scores, and how retraining on fresh data helps. The lesson is that 'statistically predictable' and 'economically exploitable' are different claims, and a credible project has to test both."

The one-sentence version: *the order book does predict short-term moves, but the prediction is worth less than the cost of acting on it unless you trade far more selectively than every signal.*

## The order book is a queue-depletion machine

An LOB is a continuous double auction. Buyers' resting limit orders (bids) and sellers' resting orders (asks) sit at discrete prices that are multiples of the **tick size**, and they are matched by **price-time priority**: a better price executes first, and at the same price the earliest order executes first ([Gould et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1012.0349); [Xu, Gould & Howison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). The best bid b and best ask a define the **spread** a − b and the **mid-price** (a + b)/2. "Level k" is the k-th best price on a side, and its **depth** is the total size resting there. A market order "eats" the best queue. When that queue is empty, the best price steps to the next level.

The tick size matters more than beginners expect. For **large-tick** assets the spread is almost always one tick, and Eisler, Bouchaud and Kockelkoren report that events changing the best price make up only about **3%** of events ([arXiv:0904.0900](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0900)). The mid can only move when a best queue is depleted, so the relative size of the two best queues is the key state variable. For **small-tick** assets the spread is several ticks wide and much of the action happens inside it. On Nasdaq, only **0.6–1.6%** of order flow lands inside the spread for large-tick stocks, versus **14–27%** for small-tick ones ([Xu, Gould & Howison, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). This is why the project's synthetic generator has a large-tick regime (spread 1 tick, mean queue 800) and a small-tick regime (spread 3 ticks, queue 120). Its validation report also includes a "small-tick transfer" check ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)).

### Queue imbalance and the micro-price turn queues into a probability

The simplest predictor is **queue imbalance**, I = (n_b − n_a)/(n_b + n_a) ∈ [−1, 1], where n_b and n_a are the best-bid and best-ask volumes. Gould and Bonart fit P(up | I) = 1/(1 + e^−(x₀ + x₁I)) on 10 Nasdaq stocks over 2014. They found a "strongly statistically significant relationship" for every stock. Binary classification improved on a coin flip by **50–60% for large-tick** and **10–30% for small-tick** stocks ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Here is a worked example (illustrative arithmetic, not from a paper): bid 900 shares, ask 100 shares gives I = +0.8. Only 100 shares must trade or cancel to lift the ask, against 900 to break the bid, so "up" is more likely.

The mid-price ignores this information. The **weighted mid**, W = I′·P_a + (1 − I′)·P_b with I′ = Q_b/(Q_b + Q_a), corrects it. It weights the ask by the *bid* size, so a heavy bid pulls the estimate toward the ask. This is exactly the **WAP** used in the Optiver competitions: WAP = (bid_price·ask_size + ask_price·bid_size)/(bid_size + ask_size) ([BulletTech writeup](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/BulletTech/BulletTech/main/docs/Main_Course/Machine_Learning/2021-08-27-Kaggle-optiver.md); [Erdős slides](https://erdosinstitute.org/_files/ugd/be9ca7_e4c07cbf1fc445d69dc482e5884016ca.pdf)). Stoikov's **micro-price** is the principled refinement: the expected long-run mid given the current imbalance and spread, P_micro = M + g(I, S), estimated with a Markov chain ([SSRN 2970694](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2970694); [code](https://github.com/sstoikov/microprice)). *Flag: the micro-price formula and the claim that it is a martingale come from background knowledge. The SSRN text could not be retrieved, so check them against the paper before quoting.* The project uses the simpler deviation (micro − mid) = I·S/2 as a feature ([README](../README.md)).

### Order-flow imbalance counts what changed, not what is resting

Queue imbalance is a snapshot. **Order-flow imbalance (OFI)**, from Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014), measures the *change* in supply and demand at the best quotes. For each quote update n:

```
e_n = 1{P^B_n >= P^B_{n-1}}·q^B_n − 1{P^B_n <= P^B_{n-1}}·q^B_{n-1}
    − 1{P^A_n <= P^A_{n-1}}·q^A_n + 1{P^A_n >= P^A_{n-1}}·q^A_{n-1}
OFI_k = Σ e_n over the interval;   ΔP_k = β·OFI_k + ε_k;   β ∝ 1/depth
```

Intuitively, a new bid, a cancelled ask and a market buy all add positive pressure. A market sell and a cancelled bid of the same size count identically. On 50 S&P 500 stocks over 10-second intervals, mid changes are linear in OFI with an average **R² of 65%**, against **32%** for trade imbalance, and the slope is roughly inversely proportional to depth ([CKS 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). *Flag: the per-event formula above is the standard published form; the PDF math was garbled during research, so cross-check it against Eq. (1) of the paper.* The key caveat for a prediction project is that **this R² is contemporaneous**: it explains price changes using order flow from the same interval.

**Multi-level OFI (MLOFI)** applies the same bookkeeping at deeper levels. Out-of-sample fit improves with every added level, by about **65–75% (large-tick) and 15–30% (small-tick)** in RMSE ([Xu, Gould & Howison](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06230)). Because per-level OFIs are highly collinear, Cont, Cucuringu and Zhang compress the top 10 levels into the first principal component, "integrated OFI". That component explains **89.06%** of the variance and raises contemporaneous out-of-sample R² from **64.64% to 83.83%**. Yet the out-of-sample R² for *one-minute-ahead* returns is **negative** for every model ([CCZ, Tables 2, 5, 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)). This explain-versus-forecast gap runs through the whole project. The project computes OFI at all 10 levels and compresses it with a **train-only PCA** to 3 components, which is the leak-free version of CCZ's recipe ([pipeline](../src/microstructure/pipeline.py)).

### VPIN measures one-sided flow but carries a contested reputation

**VPIN** (Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara 2012) measures order-flow "toxicity", meaning how badly liquidity providers are being adversely selected. Trading is split into equal-*volume* buckets (a "volume clock"), and the absolute buy–sell imbalance is averaged over the last n buckets: VPIN = Σ|V^S_τ − V^B_τ|/(n·V) ([IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v25y2012i5p1457-1493.html)). Buys and sells are classified either trade by trade or by "bulk volume classification" (V^B = V·Z(ΔP/σ)). *Flag: the exact formulas are the standard statement; the primary RFS text was not accessible, though Andersen & Bondarenko confirm the structure.* Here is a worked example (illustrative arithmetic): with bucket size 1,000 and buy volumes 500, 800 and 900, the imbalances are 0, 600 and 800, so VPIN = 1,400/3,000 ≈ 0.47.

Present VPIN with its critique attached. Andersen and Bondarenko found that VPIN reached its historical high only **after** the 2010 Flash Crash. They show it is mechanically tied to trading intensity, and that its correlation with future volatility is no better than a version using **random** trade classification ([CREATES RP 2011-50](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf); [JFM 2014](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2014/vpin-and-the-flash-crash/)). The original authors replied that the critique attacks "a methodology we do not advocate" ([rejoinder](https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/118180)). The fair summary is that VPIN is a reasonable volume-time summary of signed flow plus activity, but not a validated crash or volatility forecaster. The project computes real volume-clock VPIN from the true-aggressor trade tape, mapped causally to each snapshot (`searchsorted(..., side="right") − 1`), and uses its train-period 90th percentile as a "toxic flow" gate for passive quoting ([vpin.py](../src/microstructure/vpin.py); [strategies](../src/execution/strategies.py)).

### Adverse selection explains why any of this predicts anything

Why should order flow predict prices at all? In **Glosten–Milgrom (1985)** a market maker faces some informed traders. A buy raises the probability that the asset is worth more, so ask = E[value | buy] and bid = E[value | sell], and the spread compensates for losing to the informed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3)). In **Kyle (1985)** prices respond linearly to net order flow, Δp = λ·y, and 1/λ is market depth ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913210)). *Flag: bibliographic details for both are from background knowledge; this is standard textbook material.* The CKS finding ΔP = β·OFI with β ∝ 1/depth is the empirical, quote-level cousin of Kyle's lambda ([CKS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)). The same logic explains why passive orders are dangerous. A resting bid gets filled exactly when a seller arrives, and sellers are disproportionately informed just before the price falls. That is why the project's passive maker earns almost nothing once fills are modelled honestly.

### Realized volatility is the target in the first Optiver dataset

The Optiver Realized Volatility competition uses WAP log returns r_t = log(WAP_t/WAP_{t−1}) and **realized volatility** RV = √(Σ r_t²) over a 10-minute bucket. It scores predictions by **RMSPE** = √(mean(((y − ŷ)/y)²)), which penalizes *relative* error ([competition evaluation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/overview/evaluation)). A participant showed that RMSPE equals RMSE weighted by 1/y², and 1/y² sample weights became standard practice ([discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/250324)). The project's LightGBM uses exactly those weights ([optiver_rvp.py](../src/experiments/optiver_rvp.py)). Volatility clusters, so "next RV = current RV" is a strong naive baseline. Optiver's own tutorial reports **RMSPE 0.341** for it on train ([tutorial](https://www.kaggle.com/code/jiashenliu/introduction-to-financial-concepts-and-data)).

## One pipeline scores seven models on identical test rows

The design principle, which came out of the audit, is that **every model is evaluated identically**. That means one data-prep module, features fit on training rows only, chronologically purged splits, the same test rows for tabular and sequence models, and the same class weighting. The README states the purpose as testing "whether predictions survive realistic execution" ([README](../README.md)). The original one-line spec framed the goal as finding "the boundary between theoretical alpha and realized profitability" ([project_research.md](../project_research.md)).

### Features: 27 tabular columns and one 40-column tensor

`MicrostructurePipeline.fit()` estimates the tick size, a decimal-precision normalizer and the MLOFI PCA (3 components) **on training snapshots only**. `transform()` then emits 27 tabular features: depth imbalance at levels 1, 3, 5 and 10; spread in ticks; micro-price deviation; bid and ask book slopes; VPIN; level-1 OFI; three OFI principal components; per-level MLOFI for levels 1–5; and lags 1, 2 and 5 of OFI, L1 imbalance and micro-price deviation. Separately it emits a normalized 40-column tensor (10 levels × bid/ask × price/size) for the sequence models, using 100-step windows ([pipeline.py](../src/microstructure/pipeline.py); [feature list](../artifacts/experiment_c_feature_importance.csv)). This mirrors the field's split between engineered order-flow features and raw-book deep learning. That split matters because Kolm, Turiel and Westray found models trained on OFI "generally beat" models trained on raw book states ([Kolm et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v33y2023i4p1044-1081.html)). DeepLOB's own convolutions are explicitly designed to rebuild micro-price and imbalance ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)).

### Labels: smoothed three-class direction with a FLAT band

The synthetic label compares a 5-event trailing mean of the mid at t+k with the mid at t. It is UP or DOWN beyond **α = 1.5 bp** and FLAT otherwise, and the last k labels are set to FLAT ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)). On BTC the threshold is set per horizon to the **1/3 quantile of |smoothed k-row return| over training rows only**, so one third of training labels are FLAT ([labels.py](../src/data/labels.py); [common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)). FI-2010 keeps its published labels: the mean of the next k mids versus the current mid, with a fixed ±0.002 threshold ([Ntakaris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03233)). That threshold was picked to balance classes at one horizon, "not … in accordance with its financial implication" ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). Smoothed labels deserve suspicion. Labels at t and t+1 share k−1 future mids, so they are highly autocorrelated. Lee (2024) shows that on a smoothed AAPL target DeepLOB scores **0.659** accuracy against **0.648** for a naive forecast that just reuses the lagged target ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14157v1)). This is why the project's honest momentum baseline is so important.

### Validation: purged chronological splits and a self-checking report

Splits are chronological 70/15/15. `purged_split_indices` drops the last k rows of train and of validation, because their labels overlap the next split, and drops the final k rows of the series, where labels are undefined. Training starts at row `SEQ_LEN − 1` so sequence and tabular models train on the same rows. `LOBSequenceDataset(target_indices=…)` builds each window to end exactly at its target row, using earlier rows as causal context ([lob_dataset.py](../src/data/lob_dataset.py); [common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)). This is the "purging" idea from López de Prado: drop training samples whose label windows overlap the test labels ([Wikipedia: purged CV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation)). The robustness script adds several checks: purged cross-validation with a 1% embargo, a permutation test, MCC, Cohen's kappa, a train–test generalization gap, lift over momentum, and transfer from large-tick to small-tick data. Each check is a `(name, value, condition, criterion)` tuple whose PASS/FAIL is computed, not hard-coded ([verify script](../scripts/verify_robustness_and_leakage.py); [validation_checks.py](../src/utils/validation_checks.py)). One naming quirk: `PurgedTimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)` actually yields **4** test folds, while the README calls it 5-fold and the submission README says 4-fold ([purged_cv.py](../src/data/purged_cv.py)).

The metrics follow from the class imbalance. If 70% of labels are FLAT, always predicting FLAT scores accuracy 0.70 and weighted-F1 ≈ 0.58, but macro-F1 ≈ 0.27 and MCC = 0 (illustrative arithmetic in the [validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md); see [Chicco & Jurman](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6941312/) on MCC). The project's synthetic Naive Flat lands almost exactly there (accuracy 0.698, macro-F1 0.274). That is why **macro-F1 is the headline metric**, and why learned models with balanced class weights show *lower* accuracy than Naive Flat ([exp A](../artifacts/experiment_a_results.csv)).

DeepLOB, the reference deep model, has three stages. (1×2) stride-2 convolutions first pair each price with its volume, then combine bid and ask, "actually form[ing] the micro-price". A (1×10) convolution integrates the levels. Inception modules then learn "different moving averages", and a 64-unit LSTM models time. The whole network has about 60k parameters ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). TLOB alternates temporal self-attention across snapshots with "spatial" self-attention across features, after a Bilinear Normalization (BiN) layer ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). **A caveat to raise when presenting:** the research notes' reading of the project's `tlob.py` is that it projects the 40 features to d_model and then attends only **across time steps**, not across the feature axis. If so, it is not a faithful dual-attention TLOB ([tlob.py](../src/models/tlob.py)). *Flag: this is a code reading from the research notes; the README does not mention it.*

### The execution simulator pays for every shortcut academic papers take

The simulator ([simulator.py](../src/execution/simulator.py); [strategies.py](../src/execution/strategies.py)) handles execution as follows. An aggressive order arrives at the first snapshot whose timestamp is ≥ t + latency. It walks up to 10 levels of that snapshot's book (overflow fills at the last level ± $0.05), pays the taker fee, and has its slippage measured against the decision-time mid. A passive order rests at the touch and fills only on a **trade-through**, meaning the best bid must trade *below* the order's price within 50 events. That is a conservative back-of-queue assumption, and it earns the maker rebate. The aggressive taker sends one lot per UP/DOWN signal up to a ±500-share inventory cap, with **no exit logic**. The passive maker keeps at most one working order and cancels when the signal flips, VPIN exceeds its train 90th percentile, or 50 events pass. An "academic" regime trades at the mid with no costs. That is the frictionless "fantasy" benchmark, matching DeepLOB's own simulation using "mid-prices without transaction costs" ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)).

These choices match the literature's fill hierarchy, which runs from optimistic touch-fill, through conservative trade-through, to explicit queue-position simulation ([Moallemi & Yuan](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25461/queue-value-2016.pdf); [Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)). The underlying cost arithmetic is simple. A taker pays about the half-spread plus the fee on every fill, so a round trip costs about the full spread plus twice the fee. For symmetric ±m moves with round-trip cost c, the breakeven hit rate is p* = (m + c)/(2m), which exceeds 1 whenever c > m (illustrative formula in the [execution notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md), see [Binance fee schedule](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/trading) for fee magnitudes). Synthetic parameters are $1M capital, 100-share lots, −0.5 bp maker / 1.5 bp taker, and 1 ms base latency. BTC uses 0.1 BTC lots, ±0.5 BTC, 0 bp maker / 4 bp taker ("Coinbase Pro fee schedule (April 2021), top volume tier"), and a latency grid in whole seconds ([common.py](../src/experiments/common.py); [README](../README.md)).

### The latency benchmark measures a stand-in, not the trained model

Two latency measurements exist, and you should keep them apart. Experiment D measures **single-sample inference of the trained models**: the LightGBM raw booster takes **0.138 ms** on CPU, and DeepLOB takes **2.365 ms** on CUDA at batch 1 (median across seeds) ([inference latency](../artifacts/experiment_d_inference_latency.csv)). The C++20 benchmark times feature extraction, tree inference and a simulated order on 100,000 random-walk snapshots, but its tree ensemble is **randomly generated** (50 trees × 3 classes, depth 6, random thresholds). It is not the trained LightGBM, and its C++ features are not checked against Python ([main_benchmark.cpp](../cpp/src/main_benchmark.cpp)). It reports **399k snapshots/s**, tree inference P50 1.8 µs, and end-to-end P99 6.3 µs. Commit `b4bafb7` separately claims a 2.41× speedup to **271 ns** per event from SoA + AVX2 + branchless trees, with no saved CSV ([C++ CSV](../artifacts/cpp_latency_benchmarks.csv)). The README is honest that these "are not the latency of the model evaluated above, and they do not include network or market-data parsing" ([README](../README.md)).

Every long run checkpoints at a natural boundary. ## Five datasets each test a different claim

Each dataset was chosen to test a different claim, so they should not be read as five repetitions of one experiment.

**Synthetic generator: a lab with a planted mechanism.** The generator has 10 levels and exponential inter-arrival times averaging 10 ms. Its event mix is 40% market orders, 40% limit orders and 20% cancellations. An informed burst starts at random (probability 0.08 in the large-tick regime) and lasts **15–59 events**. During a burst, market orders are 85/15 in the informed direction, limit insertions favour that side 70/30, and cancellations hit the opposite side ([generator](../src/data/synthetic_generator.py)). Each seed produces 60,000 events (~10 simulated minutes), and results average 5 seeds ([README](../README.md)). Because the mechanism is known, the generator can show whether models find it. Results therefore describe the generator, not markets. Note also that the docstring's claim of "Hawkes self-exciting order arrival" does not match the code, which uses i.i.d. exponential arrivals, and that a `noise_volatility` parameter is defined but never used.

**Coinbase BTC-USD: real fees on real flow.** This is the Kaggle "High Frequency Crypto Limit Order Book Data" set (martinsn, CC0): **1,030,728** one-second rows from 7 to 19 April 2021, with 15 levels per side, of which the loader uses 10. Prices are rebuilt from distance-to-mid columns. A trade tape with true aggressor side is reconstructed from per-second buy and sell notional ([crypto_loader.py](../src/data/crypto_loader.py)). The data is cut into **4 contiguous segments of 257,682 rows** (~3 days each), each with its own 70/15/15 split. k is measured in 1-second rows, so k=20 means 20 seconds ([common.py](../src/experiments/common.py)).

**FI-2010: the published-benchmark sanity check.** FI-2010 covers five Finnish stocks on Nasdaq Nordic over 10 days in June 2010, one row per 10 events, with 144 features (40 raw LOB + 104 handcrafted) ([Ntakaris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03233)). The project uses **Setup 2**: train on days 1–7 and test on days 8–10, the same files DeepLOB's public code uses. It uses **no class weights**, and its headline F1 is support-weighted, to match DeepLOB's tables ([fi2010_benchmark.py](../src/experiments/fi2010_benchmark.py)). Using weighted F1 is deliberate. In DeepLOB's tables recall equals accuracy in every row, which is the mathematical signature of support-weighted averaging ([DeepLOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668)). FI-2010 has known problems: it is small, old, pre-normalized, and labelled with a threshold chosen for class balance ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)).

**Optiver Realized Volatility (2021): a leakage case study.** The task is to predict next-10-minute RV per (stock_id, time_id) from 10 minutes of 2-level book and trade data. time_ids were shuffled, and the host said a shuffled time_id "will not contain any information" ([host FAQ](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/249752)). The 1st-place solution (nyanp) recovered real prices as 0.01 / (smallest normalized tick), then recovered **time order** with a 1-D t-SNE of the time_id × stock price matrix. It then built nearest-neighbour features from similar time_ids, which took the score "from 0.21 to 0.19" ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). The project copies the order recovery, runs **forward expanding-window CV** (4 folds of 10%) on the recovered order, and compares three versions of the neighbour features: none, past-only, and transductive (the winner's version, which reaches into the future). It adds a shuffled K-fold run for contrast ([optiver_rvp.py](../src/experiments/optiver_rvp.py)).

**Optiver Trading at the Close (2023): online learning under drift.** The task is to predict each stock's 60-second WAP move relative to a synthetic Nasdaq index, in basis points, during the 10-minute closing auction, scored by MAE. The data covers 200 stocks, 55 snapshots per day and 481 training days ([data page](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/data); [index weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851)). The project follows the 1st-place split: train on date_id < 400 and hold out the last 81 days. It builds 93 features, including the winner's "magic" seconds-bucket-group and cross-sectional ratio and rank features plus the previous day's revealed target. It compares a zero baseline, a static LightGBM (MAE objective, 255 leaves), and the same model **retrained every 12 holdout days**. Post-processing subtracts the cross-sectional mean prediction, with equal weights because the index weights are not in `train.csv` ([optiver_close.py](../src/experiments/optiver_close.py)).

Horizons are **not like-for-like across datasets**. A synthetic event is ~10 ms (k=20 ≈ 200 ms), a BTC row is 1 s (k=20 = 20 s), and an FI-2010 row is 10 events. Never put the synthetic, BTC and FI-2010 "k=20" numbers on one axis as if they were the same horizon.

## Ten audit bugs had manufactured the original headline

The first version was built on 23–24 October 2026. Its README claimed "a fast C++ decision tree ensemble (LightGBM) achieves higher realized net Sharpe ratios than mathematically superior but computationally slower deep learning models" ([pre-audit README via `git show 7767a7d^`](../README.md)). An audit (artifacts regenerated 26 October, committed as `7767a7d` on 30 October) found that most of the evidence for that claim was produced by bugs. If you present this project, this table is the most instructive part, because each row is a pitfall that published work has also fallen into.

| # | Bug | Symptom | Fix | Guarding test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Momentum baseline used y[t−1], which already contains k−1 future events | Momentum macro-F1 **0.9646**, far above LightGBM 0.6039 | Predict y[t−k], the last *realized* label → 0.519 | `test_momentum_uses_only_realized_labels` |
| 2 | "DeepLOB" latency curve was LightGBM signals rerun with +4 ticks of delay | Fabricated speed-vs-accuracy chart | Exp A returns real DeepLOB predictions; Exp D executes them; inference measured | — |
| 3 | VPIN without timestamps was `clip(|imbalance_l1|·0.5 + 0.3)` | "VPIN" was a copy of L1 imbalance | Generator emits timestamps; real volume-clock VPIN; NaN if no tape | `test_pipeline_uses_real_vpin_and_tick_spread` |
| 4 | float32 prices + `+= tick` left rounding residue, making `rel_spread` a proxy for price level | `rel_spread` was LightGBM's **top feature** (14.14%) | float64, tick-grid snapping, integer `spread_ticks` (now 0.0% gain) | same as above |
| 5 | Sharpe annualized per ~10 ms event as if each were a second | Sharpe **168.67** (taker), **556.89** (passive) on ~90 s | Resample to wall-clock bars; README stops reporting Sharpe | — |
| 6 | PCA and normalizer fit on the full series | Test information leaked into features | `fit(lob[:train_end])`, purged splits | `test_pipeline_fits_on_training_rows_only` |
| 7 | LightGBM class-balanced; LR and deep nets unweighted | Macro-F1 compared different objectives | Balanced weights everywhere | — |
| 8 | Passive orders always filled immediately | Passive maker earned **$1,096**, more than the $908 fantasy regime | Trade-through rule within 50 events → ~$0 PnL | `test_passive_fill_requires_trade_through` |
| 9 | Validation report wrote hard-coded "PASS" strings | "Zero Leakage Verified" regardless of values | Computed (value, criterion) checks; permutation test described honestly | — |
| 10 | Latency counted in events, not time | Latency had no physical meaning | `searchsorted(timestamps, t + latency_ms)` | `test_latency_uses_timestamps` |

Sources: diffs and pre-audit artifacts in commit `7767a7d` as summarized in [this_project notes, section 2](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/this_project.md); [baselines.py](../src/models/baselines.py); [simulator.py](../src/execution/simulator.py); [metrics.py](../src/execution/metrics.py); [tests](../tests). The test count rose from 12 to 24. *Flag: a "−0.999 correlation" between rel_spread and price level was quoted in an earlier brief, but the audit script's output was not saved, so that figure cannot be verified from files.* The **eleventh** issue, found later, was the untracked `labels.py` described above, now fixed in `6a6bcd5`.

Three of these bugs are worth explaining carefully, because they generalize. **Bug 1 is label overlap in disguise.** With k-step smoothed labels, the label at t−1 shares almost all of its future window with the label at t, so "repeat the previous label" is a near-copy of the answer. Lee (2024) found the same effect in published work: a naive lagged-target forecast reached 0.648 accuracy against DeepLOB's 0.659 ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14157v1)). **Bug 4 is the classic "illegitimate feature".** The model memorized the price level through a float rounding artifact. It looked like microstructure skill and was nothing of the kind. **Bug 5 is the Sharpe trap.** Lo (2002) shows that serial correlation alone can overstate annualized Sharpe by up to 65% ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2002/the-statistics-of-sharpe-ratios)). Annualizing 90 seconds of event-level P&L multiplies a noisy estimate by an enormous √(periods per year), which is why the README now says such numbers "mean nothing" ([README](../README.md)). After the fix the synthetic CSVs still contain 100 ms-bar annualized Sharpes (e.g. 636.5 ± 359.7). Do not quote them.

The net effect reversed the project's story. Without the leaky baseline, learned models add about **0.04–0.07 macro-F1** over an honest naive rule rather than losing to it. Without the fake curve, LightGBM's profit advantage over DeepLOB appears at **zero latency**, so it comes from better signals, not from speed.

## The project lands where careful literature lands, not where hype does

**DeepLOB and FI-2010.** The project's 82.88% k=10 accuracy sits much closer to DeepLOB's published 84.47% than to independent re-runs. TLOB re-ran DeepLOB at **71.1** F1 at h=10, and LOBCAST measured **71.4 ± 5.3** average F1 on FI-2010 against a claimed 78.9 ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757); [LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). One plausible explanation is the metric. The project matches DeepLOB's support-weighted F1 and no-class-weight setup, while TLOB and LOBCAST do not state their averaging ([deep-learning notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/deep_learning_lob_benchmarks.md)). *This explanation is an inference, not a verified finding.* The project's own macro-F1 at k=10 is 0.72, which shows how much weighted-F1 flatters results when FLAT is 71% of the test set. The more important literature result is that FI-2010 scores do not transfer. On 2021–2022 Nasdaq data every model in LOBCAST fell to **48–61% F1**, and plain MLP and CNN models came within a few points of the best ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). On FI-2010, scores *rise* with horizon, the opposite of real NASDAQ and BTC data ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Treat the FI-2010 result as a check that the implementation is sound, not as evidence about markets.

**Trees versus deep nets.** No verified peer-reviewed LOB benchmark pits LightGBM against DeepLOB/TLOB on identical splits ([deep-learning notes, section 9](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/deep_learning_lob_benchmarks.md)). The project's synthetic and BTC rankings (trees ≥ deep nets) are therefore a small contribution in themselves, consistent with Grinsztajn et al.'s finding that tree models remain state of the art on medium-sized tabular data ([arXiv 2207.08815](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)). They are also consistent with the Optiver RVP winner's own ablation, where a **single LightGBM** scored 0.19699 private and would have won alone, with the conclusion "feature diversity was more important than model diversity" ([ablation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/302626)). FI-2010 is the counter-example, which makes the honest summary "it depends on the data". It is not "trees win".

**BTC against TLOB's BTC results.** TLOB reports Binance BTCUSDT F1 falling from **74.7 at ~1 s to 41.49 at ~10 s**, with DeepLOB at 68.07 → 37.43 ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The project's LightGBM at 10 s scores 0.558 macro-F1 on Coinbase. That looks better than TLOB at 10 s, but **the numbers are not comparable**. The exchange (Coinbase spot vs Binance perpetual), year (2021 vs 2023), sampling (1 s vs 250 ms), label threshold (train 1/3-quantile vs TLOB's θ) and F1 averaging all differ. What does transfer is the shape: steep decay with horizon, and small gaps between architectures. TLOB itself tested a cost-aware threshold only on TSLA (F1 falling to 41.39 / 36.48 / 30.82). It could not apply it to Intel, where 99.99% of labels became "stable", and it ran no profit backtest ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The BTC experiment supplies exactly that missing step, and finds the edge smaller than the fee.

**Predictability versus profitability.** This is the project's main thesis, and it matches the best papers. Lucchese, Pakkanen and Veraart find predictability "ubiquitous" but lasting only **50–300 book updates**, measured on mid-to-mid returns that "are not tradable in practice" ([arXiv 2211.13777](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777)). Kolm et al. put the effective horizon at about **two average price changes** ([Kolm et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v33y2023i4p1044-1081.html)). Briola, Bartolucci and Aste conclude that "traditional machine learning metrics fail to adequately assess the quality of forecasts" in LOBs, and they find DeepLOB near chance on small-tick stocks (MCC **0.01–0.04** at 50–100 updates) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315853/)). LOBCAST's trading simulation found profitability "far from guaranteed" ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). The project adds a quantitative bridge between the two literatures, showing where on the cost axis the edge disappears.

**Optiver RVP against the leaderboard.** The project's numbers sit on a different scale from the leaderboard and should not be ranked against it. They come from forward CV on training data with an early-stopping bias, while Kaggle scored a private, later period. Directionally, though, they line up. The project's naive 0.337 matches Optiver's 0.341 ([tutorial](https://www.kaggle.com/code/jiashenliu/introduction-to-financial-concepts-and-data)). Its base LightGBM at 0.233 is near the winner's no-neighbour LightGBM at **0.22492** (rank #1358). Its leaky transductive 0.213 points toward the winner's **0.19548**. The winner's own ablation shows that "tick-size leak-free" volatility and trade-size neighbours alone reach **0.20367**, while real-price neighbours push the score to #1 ([ablation](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/302626)). nyanp himself wrote, "I don't think we can use future information in a real Optiver's scenario" ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). The hosts briefly added noise to weaken the trick, which crashed many top teams (nyanp went from 2nd to 3836th), and then reverted it ([noise update](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/282791); [revert](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/284121)). The project's contribution here is to measure how much of the leaderboard gap is future leakage, on the winner's own pipeline.

**Optiver Close against the winner.** The 1st-place solution (hyd) blended **CatBoost 0.5 / GRU 0.3 / Transformer 0.2** on 300 features. It trained on the first 400 days with the last 81 held out, **retrained every 12 days** during the forecasting phase, and subtracted the index-weighted mean prediction. It scored **5.4030** private MAE ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/487446)). Its ablation is the benchmark for the project's pending result. Online learning moved the blend from **5.4438 to 5.4030**, about 0.04 MAE, which exceeds the gap between 1st and 10th place (5.4030 vs 5.4379) ([leaderboard](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/leaderboard)). The all-zeros submission scored **5.465** on the public period ([Pims](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/443195)), so even the winner improved on zero by only about 1.1%. That comparison is approximate because the public and private periods differ. The project differs from the winner in two known ways: LightGBM only, and equal-weighted rather than index-weighted demeaning. The index weights are recoverable by regression with R² ≈ 0.99999999 ([weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851)).

## Limitations and pitfalls to state before anyone asks

The README lists six limitations ([README](../README.md)). Results so far are mostly synthetic. Synthetic test windows are ~90 s, so PnL is noisy. The strategy is one lot per signal with no confidence sizing and no exit logic. The book does not react to the project's own orders. Deep models get ≤10 epochs with little tuning. The C++ benchmark runs a random ensemble. To these, add the issues the research notes found in code. TLOB attends only over time, if the code reading is right. RVP early-stops on the scored fold. GPU nondeterminism moves DeepLOB results. The generator's docstring over-claims Hawkes arrivals. Book-walk overflow uses a fixed ±$0.05 that is negligible at a $60k BTC price. The BTC "1 ms" base latency actually means "the next 1-second snapshot". FI-2010 test windows can cross stock boundaries, which is inherited from the benchmark files. `src/config.py` holds vestigial parameters that nothing uses ([this_project notes, section 7](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/this_project.md)).

Several deeper pitfalls belong in any talk. **Replay cannot capture your own impact.** If a simulated market order consumes the ask, the next replayed snapshot still shows the historical ask, as if you had never traded. Replay P&L is an upper bound that is valid only for orders much smaller than top-of-book depth ([JAX-LOB](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.13289); [Tóth et al. on square-root impact](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694v3)). **One-second snapshots hide sub-second structure**: which side traded first, quote flickers, and the latency race that Budish, Cramton and Shim show is won by the fastest firm ([BCS](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69665.pdf)). **Effective sample size is far smaller than row count.** With horizon-h overlapping labels, N_eff ≈ N/h. *(Flag: this is a standard time-series approximation stated from memory in the [validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md).)* So 38,633 BTC test rows at k=20 behave like roughly 2,000 independent observations, and per-seed standard deviations understate uncertainty. **Multiple testing** applies too. Every horizon, threshold, feature set and architecture tried counts as a trial. With many trials, the best backtest is expected to look good by chance. That motivates the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting ([Bailey & López de Prado, DSR](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551); [PBO](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)), and Harvey, Liu and Zhu's t > 3 hurdle ([NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)). **The permutation test only catches misalignment.** As the project now says honestly, it "cannot prove that features don't look ahead" ([README](../README.md)). **VPIN may be proxying activity.** Andersen and Bondarenko's critique implies VPIN's 7.5–9.6% gain share should be checked against volume and trade-count features ([A&B](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf)). **Displayed imbalance can be spoofed**, a live-trading risk that offline datasets cannot reveal ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=27752)). **"Shuffled" data is rarely shuffled.** Optiver's time_ids were reordered within weeks ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-realized-volatility-prediction/discussion/274970)). Finally, fee schedules are a moving target. The project's 4 bp BTC taker fee is the April 2021 top tier. Current retail schedules are far higher (Binance Regular 10 bp taker), and current Coinbase tiers are *unverified*, with secondary sources in conflict ([Binance](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/trading); [Altrady](https://www.altrady.com/blog/crypto-trading-platforms/coinbase-advanced-trade)). The BTC loss is therefore, if anything, optimistic for a retail trader.

## What to try next, in priority order

The ordering below puts first whatever answers the project's own open question ("when, if ever, does the edge survive costs?") most cheaply with data already on disk. Priorities are a synthesis of the notes, not a sourced ranking.

| Priority | Experiment | Effort | Why now |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Finish runs; add paired tests, block bootstrap, per-day metrics; fix RVP early-stopping-on-test | Days, CPU | Makes every existing number defensible |
| P1 | Selective trading: accuracy/PnL-coverage curves, calibration, conformal abstention on BTC | Low, CPU | Directly attacks the 5× fee gap |
| P2 | Cost-aware and triple-barrier labels evaluated by simulator PnL | Low–medium | Fills the gap TLOB left open |
| P3 | Optiver Close: retrain-schedule ablation, recovered index weights, Diebold–Mariano test | Low | Replicates the winner's ablation honestly |
| P4 | Model fairness: faithful dual-attention TLOB, MLPLOB, BiN-CTABL, queue-imbalance logit baseline, more epochs | Medium, GPU | Removes "under-trained / mis-implemented" objections |
| P5 | Benchmark the *trained* LightGBM in C++ (TL2cgen, lleaves) with feature parity | Low–medium | Fixes the random-ensemble weakness |
| P6 | VPIN-vs-intensity ablation and TreeSHAP | Low | Tests a contested feature |
| P7 | Multi-horizon regression (Kolm-style) and BTC→ETH cross-OFI | Low–medium | Literature replication on owned data |
| P8 | Regime-aware retraining on BTC | Low–medium | Non-stationarity is the Close lesson |
| Stretch | Event-level data → Hawkes/queue-reactive simulator → AS market making / RL | High | Needed for credible passive strategies |

**P0: make the existing numbers defensible.** Let BTC segments 1–3 and the FI-2010 horizons finish, then report the BTC four-segment mean ± std. For every model pair, report per-segment and per-day *paired* differences with a Wilcoxon or paired t-test, and a block bootstrap with block length ≥ k. These are the protocols recommended in the validation notes. Henderson et al. and Bouthillier et al. are cited there for seed variance (*flag: those two references were not fetched*) ([validation notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/validation_and_execution.md)). Fix RVP by carving an inner validation slice from each training window for early stopping, so the scored fold is untouched. Rerun the shuffled K-fold with the same feature set as the forward variants, to remove the `stock_rv_target_ratio` confound. Keep a running count of every configuration tried, so a Deflated-Sharpe-style correction is possible later ([DSR](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)).

**P1: trade only when confident.** The BTC taker loses because it acts on all ~16,000 signals. The cheapest test is an **accuracy-coverage and PnL-coverage curve**. Rank test rows by max class probability, then for coverage levels {100%, 50%, 20%, 10%, 5%, 2%, 1%} run the existing simulator only on that top slice. Plot net PnL per trade against coverage, with a day-level bootstrap CI. Add calibration first: isotonic regression for LightGBM and temperature scaling for DeepLOB, fitted on the time-ordered validation split, with reliability diagrams and ECE. Then wrap predictions in **adaptive conformal inference**, α_{t+1} = α_t + γ(α − err_t), with γ ∈ {0.005, 0.01, 0.05}, and trade only when the prediction set is a singleton UP or DOWN ([Gibbs & Candès](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170); [Zaffran et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07282)). *Flag: these conformal references are background knowledge, not re-fetched.* The success criterion is concrete. A positive net PnL per trade at some coverage level, with a bootstrap CI excluding zero across all four segments, would be the project's first evidence of a tradable edge. A null result is equally publishable. Add exit logic (hold for k rows, then flatten) at the same time, because the current strategy never closes positions.

**P2: change the target to what a trader is paid for.** Replace "smoothed mid moved more than θ" with a **tradable label**. UP if the best bid at t+h minus the current best ask exceeds the round-trip fee; DOWN symmetrically; FLAT otherwise. Report the class balance per dataset (expect FLAT to dominate on BTC, as TLOB found for Intel) and evaluate by simulator PnL and hit rate on trades taken, not F1 ([new-directions notes](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/new_directions.md); [TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Two variants follow. The first is a **TLOB-label ablation**: decouple the smoothing window k from horizon h (k ∈ {1, 5, 10, h} at fixed h) and show whether F1 and PnL move together. TLOB did not test profitability, so this fills a documented gap ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). The second is **triple-barrier labels with meta-labelling**, where a second model decides whether to act on LightGBM's signal ([López de Prado, AFML](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Advances+in+Financial+Machine+Learning-p-9781119482086); *flag: background reference*). Triple-barrier labels overlap, so keep purging.

**P3: finish Optiver Close as a proper ablation.** Once the static and retrained MAEs exist, add three things. (a) Retrain schedules of 0, 1 and 6 updates, mirroring the winner's 0/1/5-update table, where the blend went 5.4438 → 5.4157 → 5.4030 ([1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/487446)). (b) **Recovered index weights**: regress the implied index return on the 200 stock returns over training days, then use index-weighted rather than equal-weighted demeaning, as the 1st and 9th place did ([weights post](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/442851); [9th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/486868)). (c) A Diebold–Mariano test or day-level block bootstrap on daily MAE differences, because the whole leaderboard spread is a few hundredths of a basis point. An optional fourth step is LightGBM warm-start (`init_model`) and `refit` with decay, both used by the 5th-place team ([5th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/optiver-trading-at-the-close/discussion/462743)).

**P4: give the deep models a fair fight.** Rewrite TLOB so one attention block runs over the feature axis and one over time, per the paper. Add MLPLOB, which roughly ties TLOB on FI-2010 (81.64 vs 81.55 at h=10), suggesting attention is not the key ingredient ([TLOB](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15757)). Add BiN-CTABL, the most robust model in LOBCAST at 61.2 F1 on LOB-2021 ([LOBCAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01915)). Add a **one-feature queue-imbalance logistic regression** as a literature-grounded baseline that any deep model must beat ([Gould & Bonart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03492)). Train deep models for 30–50 epochs with patience ≥ 5 and fix seeds, and report whether the synthetic and BTC rankings survive. If LightGBM still wins, the "trees win on engineered features" claim gets much stronger. If not, the project learns something important.

**P5: benchmark the model you actually trained.** Save the BTC LightGBM `model.txt`. Compile it with **TL2cgen** (C99 code, built with `-O3 -march=native` into the existing C++ harness) and **lleaves** (LLVM), and compare against the LightGBM C API single-row path and ONNX Runtime with one thread. Measure p50/p99/p99.9 at batch 1 over 10⁵–10⁶ *real* test feature rows, check that the backends agree numerically (max abs diff), and port the Python feature computation to C++ with a parity test. The vendor benchmark shows lleaves at 9.61 µs vs LightGBM 52.31 µs at batch 1 on old hardware ([lleaves](https://siboehm.com/articles/21/lleaves); [TL2cgen docs](https://tl2cgen.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html)). Even so, the project's own latency sweep shows that microseconds do not matter for a 1-second-snapshot strategy. Frame this as engineering hygiene, not as alpha.

**P6: interrogate the features.** Add raw volume and trade-count features, then ablate VPIN to test whether its 9.6% gain share on BTC survives once trading intensity is available. Andersen and Bondarenko predict it largely will not ([A&B](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_50.pdf)). Replace split-gain attribution with **TreeSHAP** (`pred_contrib=True`), grouping correlated OFI levels before interpreting, and compare attributions across horizons ([Lundberg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04610); *flag: background reference*).

**P7: replicate two literature results on data you own.** First, a **multi-horizon regression** in the style of Kolm et al.: predict log mid-returns at h ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100} rows, comparing OFI inputs against raw-book inputs. Report out-of-sample R² and Spearman IC per horizon, and expect tiny R² with a peak at short horizons ([Kolm et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3900141)). Second, **BTC→ETH cross-OFI**. The Kaggle crypto dataset reportedly also holds ETH and ADA (*flag: confirmed only via a third-party mirror; same timestamps and sampling not confirmed*) ([mirror](https://baselight.app/u/kaggle/dataset/martinsn_high_frequency_crypto_limit_order_book_data)). Add lagged BTC integrated OFI at 1, 2, 5 and 10 s to an ETH model and test CCZ's pattern: contemporaneous cross-impact adds nothing, lagged cross-impact helps briefly ([CCZ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.13213)).

**P8: treat non-stationarity as an experimental variable.** Using the four BTC segments as a natural time series, compare four set-ups: a static model trained on segment 0 and tested on segments 1–3; expanding-window retraining; sliding-window retraining; and LightGBM warm-start updates. Report per-day metrics as a time series rather than one pooled number. Optionally add filtered (never smoothed) HMM regime probabilities from rolling volatility, spread and OFI variance ([new-directions notes, section 6](../research_notes/LOB%20prediction%20project%20explainer/new_directions.md)). This carries the Optiver Close lesson over to crypto.

**Stretch: make passive strategies credible.** The passive maker is the strategy that could plausibly beat fees, and it is also the one the current data can model least well. Record one to two weeks of Coinbase `level2` and `matches` WebSocket data, or download a free event-level day from tardis.dev (*flag: free-day policy unconfirmed*) ([tardis docs](https://docs.tardis.dev/api)). Fit a **queue-reactive model**, in which order-arrival rates depend on queue sizes, to simulate passive fills by queue position rather than by trade-through ([Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)). On top of that, test **Avellaneda–Stoikov market making** with the reservation price r = s − q·γ·σ²·(T − t), shifted by the model's forecast ([Avellaneda & Stoikov](https://www.math.nyu.edu/~avellane/HighFrequencyTrading.pdf); *flag: background reference*). Report PnL, inventory variance, fill rate and post-fill adverse selection. Deep RL execution, generative simulators (TRADES/DeepMarket, LOBS5) and time-series foundation models are further out. For the last of these, the one verified finance study found zero-shot Chronos at **−1.37%** out-of-sample R² with directional accuracy just above 51% ([Rahimikia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18578v1); *flag: verified via search snippet only*), so treat it as a cheap negative-result baseline at most.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Limit order book (LOB) | The exchange's list of resting buy (bid) and sell (ask) orders at each price |
| Level / depth | The k-th best price on a side / the total size resting there |
| Tick size | The minimum price increment |
| Large-tick vs small-tick | Spread almost always one tick (queues decide moves) vs several ticks (moves happen inside the spread) |
| Spread / mid-price | Best ask − best bid / their average |
| Queue imbalance | (bid size − ask size)/(bid size + ask size) at the best quotes |
| Weighted mid / WAP | Best prices weighted by the *opposite* side's size; Optiver's price proxy |
| Micro-price | Expected long-run mid given imbalance and spread (Stoikov) |
| OFI / MLOFI / integrated OFI | Net change in best-quote supply and demand / the same per level / first principal component across levels |
| VPIN | Average buy–sell volume imbalance over equal-volume buckets; a "toxicity" gauge |
| Volume clock | Measuring time in traded volume rather than seconds |
| Adverse selection / toxic flow | Losing to better-informed counterparties; flow that does this to market makers |
| Taker / maker | Crosses the spread with a market order and pays a fee / rests a limit order and may earn a rebate |
| Book walking | A large market order consuming several price levels |
| Trade-through fill | A passive order counts as filled only once the price trades beyond it |
| Slippage | Execution price minus the decision-time mid |
| Fantasy / academic regime | Trading at the mid with no costs; an upper bound |
| Horizon k | How far ahead the label looks: events (synthetic), 1 s rows (BTC), 10-event rows (FI-2010) |
| Smoothed label / FLAT band α | Label from an averaged future mid; moves smaller than α count as FLAT |
| Leakage | Information unavailable at prediction time reaching the model |
| Transductive features | Features computed using the test set or future rows (e.g. RVP neighbours) |
| Purging / embargo | Dropping training rows whose labels overlap test labels / an extra buffer after the test fold |
| Macro-F1 / weighted-F1 | Unweighted mean of per-class F1 / support-weighted mean (tracks the majority class) |
| MCC / Cohen's kappa | Chance-corrected agreement scores; 0 for constant or random predictions |
| RMSPE / MAE | Root-mean-square *percentage* error (RVP) / mean absolute error (Close) |
| Realized volatility | √(Σ squared log returns) over a window |
| Setup 2 (FI-2010) | Train on days 1–7, test on days 8–10 |
| DeepLOB / TLOB / BiN | CNN-Inception-LSTM LOB model / dual-attention transformer / bilinear input normalization |
| LightGBM | Gradient-boosted decision trees |
| Online learning | Periodically retraining on newly revealed data |
| PBO / Deflated Sharpe | Probability that the in-sample winner underperforms out of sample / Sharpe adjusted for number of trials |
| Effective sample size | Number of truly independent observations, roughly N/h for overlapping h-step labels |

## Conclusion

The project's real contribution is not a model. It is a **measuring instrument**: one pipeline in which the same test rows, the same purges and the same cost model apply to every predictor, so gaps between models and between "predictable" and "profitable" can be read directly. The audit is the best evidence that this instrument matters. Before it, the project told the field's favourite story, that clever, fast models extract alpha. After it, the same code tells the story the careful literature tells: the edge is real, small, concentrated in the top of the book, and smaller than the fee. Presented this way, the leaky baseline and the fabricated latency curve are strengths of the write-up, because they show concretely how easily LOB results are manufactured.

Going forward, the open question is narrower than "can we predict prices?" It is whether a **small, confident subset** of predictions, or a passive strategy that earns rather than pays the spread, clears costs on real data with honest statistics. The BTC numbers already say where to look. A fivefold fee gap is too wide to close by better models, but it is the kind of gap that selective trading, cost-aware labels or rebate-earning quoting could plausibly close. If none of them does, that negative result will be rigorous enough to defend.
